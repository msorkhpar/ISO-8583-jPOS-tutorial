An Amount Counts Minor Units, and Its Currency Says How Many Make One

Field 4 holds 000000001500. How much money is that?

Field 4 counts the minor units of the currency in field 49, and the currency decides how many minor units make one: a dollar has 100 cents, the yen has no smaller unit, and a Kuwaiti dinar has 1,000 fils. jPOS's ISOCurrency takes the number of decimals of each ISO 4217 code from Java's currency list, then from ISOCurrency.properties files on the classpath, which can change an entry:

The opening's refund was typed as 1500 yen, and the refund code multiplied by 100, as it always had for dollars. The cardholder was credited 150,000 yen and the merchant was debited as much. The same habit in Kuwait charges 0.150 dinar for a purchase of 1.500: the merchant is paid a tenth of the price, and no cardholder complains.

toISO87String refuses 12.345 dollars instead of rounding it. A third decimal in dollars is a bug upstream, and rounding would hide it. The last line is why unit 3 keeps amounts out of double: 0.29 has no exact binary form, 0.29 * 100 is 28.999999999999996, and the cast keeps 28.

Convert once, at the edge where amounts enter or leave the switch, with the decimals of the currency the message names. Fields 5 and 6 carry their own currencies in fields 50 and 51 (Reference).

  1. Coding practice

    Send refunds in the currency's own units

    • Field 4 counts minor units of the currency in field 49, so the amount is scaled by that currency's decimals, exactly, and an amount finer than the currency allows is refused.

A Card Goes to the Destination of Its Longest Matching Prefix


A switch decides where an authorization goes from the first digits of the card number, the issuer identification number or BIN. Its routing table maps prefixes to destinations: a card network, a processor, or the bank's own authorization system for the cards it issued itself (on-us cards). Which destination gets 4111111111111111 from this table?





Many tables are keyed by the first six digits, because for decades a BIN had six. Under that lookup the bank's own cards, issued under the eight-digit 41111111, match the network's 411111 and go out to the network. The bank pays network fees to approve its own customers. When the link to the network is down, its cardholders are turned away at its own terminals, while its own host is up.


The longest matching prefix decides: try the whole number, then one digit less, until an entry matches, whatever the prefix lengths and the order of the entries.


Eight-digit BINs. ISO/IEC 7812-1 lengthened issuer identification numbers from six digits to eight in its 2017 edition, and the card networks moved over in the years after. Expect routing files that mix six- and eight-digit entries, and check which lengths your network's files carry.


The table is reference data. It changes a few times a week and every authorization reads it. Read from the database per message, every slow query delays an answer (11.3). Load it at startup, keep it in memory, and replace it whole: reload copies the new entries into a new map and swaps one reference, so a lookup sees the old table or the new one, never half of each. Card data, balances and authorization results are not reference data and don't belong in such a cache.

Capacity Is Sized From the Busiest Second, and Judged by the Slowest Answers


The year's average says nothing about the rate the switch must carry. Take the busiest hour you expect and divide by 3600. Multiply by the burst factor, the ratio of the busiest second to the average second within that hour, which last year's logs give you.


That rate times the answer time is the number of authorizations in flight at once. Each holds a session, one of the fixed number of places a switch has for requests waiting on an answer (in jPOS, the TransactionManager's limit on active transactions, unit s9), and a cardholder waits on it. For 180,000 authorizations in the busiest hour and a burst factor of 3:



The same traffic needs 45 sessions while the host answers in 0.3 seconds, and 1,200 when it answers in 8. Then the answer times, 1,000 of them from one day:



By its mean the switch is quick. The 99th percentile is 31 seconds, and terminals give up at 30. Each of those 15 purchases ends at the terminal with no answer, so its outcome is unknown and it is reversed (unit 4). Sending it again would risk a second charge, since the issuer may have approved the first. At 150 a second, 1.5% is over two reversals a second, added when the switch is busiest. Report the median and the 99th percentile next to the throughput, and compare the percentile with the terminal's timeout.


Slow answers usually come from work the answer doesn't need. Settlement files, ad-hoc reports and statement runs built from the online tables compete with authorizations for the same rows and disks. Keep on the authorization path only what decides the answer: parse, check, look up in memory, decide or forward, record, reply. Reports read a copy of the database, and batch work runs outside the online system.


A Slow Destination Gets a Limit of Its Own, and Past It an Immediate 91


A switch with 100 sessions forwards to three issuers. At the peak A gets 90 requests a second, B 45 and C 15. A and B answer in 0.3 seconds. On a Saturday C slows to 8. Whose cardholders wait?


We simulated one minute of that traffic in 10 ms steps and followed every request until it was answered. The terminal gives up after 30 seconds. First with one pool for everybody, then with at most 20 of C's requests in flight:



Each of C's requests holds a session for 8 seconds, so every second brings 135 × 0.3 + 15 × 8 = 160.5 session-seconds of work for 100 sessions. The pool fills with C's requests, and A's and B's queue behind them for longer every second. C's answers are too late from second 39, and A's and B's from second 52. A and B are healthy, their cardholders see timeouts, and each timeout becomes a reversal that adds more work.


With the limit, C can't hold more than 20 sessions, and every answer from A and B is in time. The 740 requests of C's beyond the limit are answered at once with 91, issuer or switch inoperative. None of them needs a reversal, since none reached the issuer.


Why 91 and not a decline such as 05? The issuer never saw these purchases. A decline says the issuer refused the card. 91 is an error, not a decision about the card (unit 9): the cardholder may try again in a minute, and a network that stands in for its issuers (unit 7) may decide within the limits C agreed.


Set each limit from 11.3's arithmetic. C normally has 15 × 0.3, about 5, requests in flight, so 20 leaves room for a burst and still stops a slowdown from spreading. The slot must be freed however the request ends, with an answer, a timeout or an exception. A slot kept after an exception is lost for good, and after enough of them the limit refuses everything C sends, even once C has recovered.

    Coding practice
    

    Keep one slow issuer from holding everyone's answers
    

      

    • Each destination gets its own limit of requests in flight, so a slow issuer cannot take the sessions of healthy ones.
      • 

    • A request beyond the limit is answered at once with 91, an error, not a decline, and its place is given back however the request ends.

Summary


  • An amount counts minor units, and its currency says how many make one. Multiplying by 100 pays a yen refund a hundred times over and charges a tenth of a dinar price; a double loses cents.
  • A card goes to the destination of its longest matching prefix. A six-digit lookup sends eight-digit ranges to the wrong place, such as the bank's own cards to the network. The table lives in memory and is replaced whole.
  • Capacity is sized from the busiest second and judged by the slowest answers. In-flight requests are rate times answer time. A mean hides the answers that come after the terminal's timeout, and each of those becomes a reversal.
  • A slow destination gets a limit of its own, and past it an immediate 91. Without one, a single slow issuer fills the shared sessions and every bank's terminals time out.

Challenges


  1. Your switch converts a cardholder's euros into yen for field 6. Where in that conversion can a minor unit be gained or lost, and who should get it?
  2. The network's routing file arrives every night with 400,000 prefixes. How would you check a new file before reload swaps it in, and what do you do with a file that fails the check?
  3. Last year's busiest second had 410 authorizations, and the busiest hour 290,000. What burst factor does that give, and why might next year's be higher?
  4. An issuer's normal answer time is 0.3 seconds, but it answers balance enquiries in 2. Should its limit count both kinds of request together?


Design Note: Why Field 4 Has No Decimal Point


The 1987 standard could have sent 15.00. It sends 000000001500 and leaves the decimals to the currency in field 49. A fixed-width field of digits packs the same way for every currency: twelve ASCII digits, or six bytes of BCD (unit 3), with no separator to choose between a point and a comma. Sums of minor units are exact, which matters for the totals that two institutions reconcile (unit 7).


The cost is that field 4 means nothing alone. Every program that shows, converts or compares an amount needs field 49 and a table of decimals, and both ends must use the same table. Store the currency code next to every amount you keep, or the stored amount can't be read back.
