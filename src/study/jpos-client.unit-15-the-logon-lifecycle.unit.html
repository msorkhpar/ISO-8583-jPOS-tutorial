At 02:00 the host's operators start their weekly maintenance. They send an 0800 with code 002 on link A, signing the link off from their side, and for the next twenty minutes the host refuses every sign-on. The client runs the sign-on manager and the echo responder of unit c11, with two links behind a pool that has check-enabled.

At 07:30 someone notices that since 02:00 half of all purchases have been declined with 91. Every one of them went to link A. The maintenance ended more than five hours earlier.

Unit c11 built the lifecycle a client starts: sign on, echo, sign off. The rest of it is started by the host or goes wrong on the way, in five rules, one per section from 15.1 to 15.5.

The Host Starts Network Messages Too, and Each One Needs Its Own Answer

The examples run a real Q2 with unit c11's descriptors against c11's test host on 127.0.0.1, which can now also refuse sign-ons for a while, miss an echo, answer purchases late and send its own 0800s. It declines purchases with 91 until a connection signs on, and answers echoes either way. Links A and B sit behind a round-robin pool with check-enabled. A purchase goes out about every 250 ms, and the manager echoes every 4 seconds.

The 02:00 night, compressed: host A signs off at 1.0 s and refuses sign-ons for the next 8 seconds:

The maintenance ended at 9 s. Why did nothing change?

The 002 is a request, not an answer, so QMUX hands it straight to its request listeners. c11's responder takes only echoes, so the 002 went on to <unhandled> and expired there two minutes later, unanswered. The adaptor doesn't read it either, and the connection stays open. The manager's logon mark still holds this connection's ready object, so decide says "signed on", the host answers every echo, and the enabled mark stays. The pool keeps sending A its share of the purchases, and the host declines each one, because for the host the session is over. That lasts until the logon mark expires, a day after the last sign-on.

The answer to a host's 0800 depends on what it asks:

(1) Only requests of its own kind, 0800 and its repeat 0801. A late 0210, or the host's answer to something, must go on to the next listener and to <unhandled>, as in unit c11.

(2) A list of fields, not a copy. A request can carry fields the host never expects back. A key change carries a key (15.5).

(3) A host sign-off removes both marks. Without the enabled mark, the pool sends nothing more to the link from the next request on. Without the logon mark, the manager's decide sees a connection that never signed on and signs on again. Remove only the enabled mark, and the link stays out of the pool until the logon mark expires, the same trap unit c11 showed for its own stop.

(4) A function you don't know is refused. An approval tells the host that you did what it asked. 12 stands for whatever refusal code your network's specification gives.

The handler replaces c11's responder in 20_host_mux.xml. QMUX creates it and passes it the properties:

The same night with the handler:

A left the pool the moment the 002 arrived, and no purchase was declined: B carried everything until A's sign-on was accepted at 9.6 s. The cost is a refused sign-on every second.

Hosts also send advices: an 0820 reports something that has already happened, such as a cutover to a new business day. There is nothing to refuse, so on most networks an advice and its repeat 0821 are acknowledged with an 0830 and 39=00. setResponseMTI maps both:

The handler above leaves 0820s to the next listener, so they end on <unhandled>, and a host that wants them acknowledged repeats each one as an 0821. If your host sends advices, add a branch for them.

The listener runs on the adaptor's receiver, the thread that put the message on the QMUX's <in> queue. Nothing else is read from the link while it works, so anything slow, such as a call to a hardware security module across the network, delays every answer behind it.

Networks differ. Links on ISO 8583:1993 or later use 1804 and 1814, and a function code in field 24 instead of field 70, with codes of their own. The rules of this unit stay the same.

A Refused Sign-On Waits Longer Each Time

After a refusal, wait before the next sign-on. Double the wait after each further refusal, stop at a cap, and start from the beginning after a success. Host A's sign-on lines and two of its counts on the same night, with a wait of 1 second after the first refusal and a cap of 8 seconds:

Four refusals instead of eight, at waits of 1, 2, 4 and 8 seconds. The host was ready again at 9 s, though, and A came back at 16.5 s. The cap is also the longest a link can stay out after its host has recovered.

With c11's loop, which asks on every pass, a twenty-minute maintenance window puts about 1200 refused sign-ons in the host's log, and as many lines in yours. With a wait of 5 seconds doubling to a cap of 5 minutes, it is about ten, and the link is back at most 5 minutes after the host. With another link in the pool the cap costs little; on a link that carries traffic alone, choose a smaller one.

A sign-on that gets no answer at all counts as a refusal too. Its timeout (30 seconds by default, unit c11) already spaces the attempts out, and the wait adds to it. The manager keeps two values per link: refusals in a row, and the earliest time of the next attempt. The LOGON branch of c11's step skips the link until that time, and a success clears both. Log a change of state, from signed on to refused and back, not every attempt.

Spot the Problem: Doubling by Shifting

The obvious way to double is a shift:

It passes a test with ten refusals. Does it pass one with a hundred? Beside it is a version that doubles step by step and stops at the cap, both with a base of 5 seconds and a cap of 5 minutes. The hours are the time the step-by-step version has spent waiting by then:

From refusal 52 the shift no longer fits in a long: the delay turns negative, Math.min keeps it, and the next pass signs on at once. Java uses only the low six bits of a shift count, so refusal 64 shifts by 63 and gives 0, and refusal 65 shifts by 0 and starts again at 5 seconds. Every 64 refusals the client sends a burst of sign-ons.

Double step by step instead, and stop doubling once the cap is reached. Then no refusal count can produce more than the cap.

  1. Coding practice

    Space out sign-ons while the host refuses them

    • A refused sign-on is retried after a wait that doubles with each refusal in a row up to a cap and starts over after a success; the cap is also how long a link may stay out after its host recovers.
    • Doubling by shifting overflows after a few hours of refusals and the delay turns negative, zero or short; doubling step by step inside the cap cannot.

One Lost Echo Is Not a Dead Host


c11's manager took a link out after one unanswered echo. Instead, count the failures in a row, and take the link out only at a limit. Here host A misses a single echo. The manager echoes every 3 seconds with a timeout of 2 seconds, with a limit of 1 (c11) and then 2:




With a limit of 1 a healthy link left the pool, signed on again, and came back a second later. B took the traffic for that second; with a single link, nothing could be sent in it. Echoes do get lost on a busy host, and with a limit of 1 each one costs that much.


After a failed echo the echo mark is still absent, so the next pass echoes again, not an echo interval later: failures in a row come one timeout plus one pass of the loop apart. One answered echo sets the count back to zero. A count of failures in total would sooner or later take a healthy link out over lost echoes spread across a week.


Now host A's application hangs, while 10 purchases a second go through the pool, half of them to A. With limits of 1, 2 and 3:





Until the limit is reached the link stays in the pool, and every purchase sent to it is an unknown outcome that has to be reversed (unit c12). Each failure the limit allows adds one echo timeout, plus a pass, of that. With c11's default timeout of 30 seconds and a limit of 3, that is more than a minute and a half of purchases sent to a dead host, on top of the wait for the first echo, which can be a whole echo interval.


Size the limit by what is behind the link. With another link to fail over to, use 1 or 2: a false alarm costs a second of rerouting. On a single link, taking it out stops all traffic, so allow a little more.

Drain a Link Before You Sign It Off


c11's stop takes the link out of the pool, signs it off and forgets the session, in that order, and does nothing about requests already on the link. Here Q2 is stopped for a release while a purchase waits for an answer that the host sends after 1.5 seconds. Q2 undeploys in reverse file-name order: the manager (30_) first, then the QMUX (20_), then the adaptor (10_):



Who has the money?


The host approved the purchase, and its answer found the connection closed. The caller waited out its 5-second timeout, got null, and by unit c12's rule has to reverse a purchase that went through, while the cardholder is told it failed. On a busy link a stop does that to every purchase in flight, several at each release.


So wait between taking the link out and signing it off. QMUX counts the requests still waiting for an answer: getRXPending(). Disable the link first, so nothing new arrives, then pause until that count is zero, checking before the first pause so an idle link signs off at once. Bound the wait: a request that never gets an answer counts until its own timeout ends, so the longest request timeout plus a margin is enough (QMUX can hold a request up to 10 seconds past its timeout when an answer races with it). The same stop with a drain of up to 10 seconds:



Q2 waited for the manager's stop before it undeployed the QMUX and the adaptor, so the answer came back over a live connection. The order is now disable, drain, sign off, forget. When the bound runs out with requests still pending, sign off anyway and log how many were left, since each of them is going to be reversed. To take one link out for maintenance without stopping the process, run the same four steps for that link and stop its adaptor afterwards.

A New Key Is Acknowledged Only Once It Is Installed


Unit c7 imported a PIN key received under the ZMK and checked it by its check value. On many networks that key arrives in a network message. Here the host sends an 0800 with code 161, and field 48 holds the new key under the ZMK (32 hex digits) followed by its check value (6 hex digits). The handler of 15.1 refuses code 161, so a subclass installs the key:



The security module is c7's hsm, and the ZMK, under the module's LMK, was registered at start-up by whatever loads your keys. The host sends two key changes, and the second is damaged on the way:



The 0810 with 00 is the host's signal to switch. From the moment the network defines (at once, from the next message, or after a confirmation), the host translates PINs to the new key. Answer 00 before the key is stored, and a failed import leaves the host on a key you don't have: every PIN purchase is declined 55, as in unit c7, and to the host it looks like your fault. So the handler imports, compares the check value, stores, and only then answers. When anything fails, the answer is a refusal and the old key stays, so PIN traffic continues under it.


The handler above overwrites the previous key. A host that may still send messages under it for a while needs the previous key kept as well, until no request in flight can be using it. Field 48 never goes back to the host: the answer is built from fields 7, 11 and 70 (15.1). Keep it out of the logs too, even though it is encrypted. The wipe list of the ProtectedLogListener (unit c8) covers the message, but the 2.1.7 security module logs its own s-m-operation event for every import, with the encrypted key in it, to whatever logger the hsm has.


Networks differ. Some let the client ask for a new key with an 0800 and send the key in the


  1. The code, the field, the layout and the moment the new key becomes active are all in your network's specification.

    • A key change is answered 00 only after the key is imported, its check value matches and it is stored; otherwise it is refused, the old key stays, and the key never travels back in the answer.

Summary


  • The host starts network messages too. Answer each 0800 by its function, through the source you are given and from a list of fields. A host sign-off removes the enabled and logon marks, so the pool stops at once and the manager signs on again. Unknown functions are refused; advices are acknowledged with an 0830.
  • A refused sign-on waits longer each time, doubling up to a cap and starting over after a success. The cap is also how long a link may stay out after its host recovers. A shift that doubles overflows after a few hours of outage.
  • One lost echo is not a dead host. Count failures in a row. Each failure the limit allows keeps a dead host in the pool for one more echo timeout, and every purchase sent there is reversed.
  • Drain before you sign off: disable, wait (bounded) until getRXPending() is zero, sign off, forget. A stop without the drain turns approvals in flight into reversals.
  • A new key is acknowledged only once it is installed: import, compare the check value, store, then answer 00. A failure is refused and the old key stays.

QMUX.getRXPending() is the number of requests waiting for an answer on that QMUX, from request and from asynchronous requests alike. It drops when the answer arrives or the request's timeout ends.


Challenges


  1. Your host answers echoes in 200 ms on a normal day and in up to 12 seconds during its batch window at 23:00. Choose the echo timeout and the limit for a pool of two links, and for a single link. What does each choice cost at 23:00, and on the night the host really dies?
  2. Forty clients sign on to the same host, all with the same base and cap. The host restarts. Draw when their sign-ons arrive. What would adding a random part to each wait change, and how would you test it without a clock?
  3. A release stops Q2 with drain-time 90 seconds while the host is hung. What does the operator see during those 90 seconds, and what would you log at the end so the reversals that follow are no surprise?
  4. A host changes the PIN key every day at 02:00, and its 0810 means "switch now". List the messages in flight at that moment whose PIN blocks are under the old key, and decide how long the client keeps that key.


Design Note: Why Management Travels With the Payments


The 0800s share the link with the purchases: the same adaptor, the same QMUX, the same STAN sequence and the same host application. Much of this unit is the price of that choice. A slow echo looks the same as a slow purchase, a sign-off can overtake a purchase that went out first, and a key change arrives in the middle of traffic that depends on the old key.


A separate management channel would avoid that, but for the lifecycle of a link the shared path is the point. An echo that comes back proves that the path a purchase takes can carry a message right now, which is what the pool needs to know. An echo on another channel would prove only that the other channel works. A sign-on, too, is tied to the connection it travels on, which is what lets unit c11 compare one object with another to know which connection is signed on.
