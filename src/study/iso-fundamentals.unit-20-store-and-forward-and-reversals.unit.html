At 19:20 on a Friday the acquirer's link to the card network drops, and it stays down for 40 minutes. 37 purchases were in flight and got no answer, so 37 reversals wait to go out. Behind them, the fuel pumps report 212 completions while the link is down. At 20:00 the link is back. What does the acquirer send, in what order and how fast, and what does the issuer do with a completion it receives twice? Six rules, one per section heading, hold for any implementation; units c12 and s12 show them in jPOS.

A SAF Holds Reports of Decisions Already Taken, Never a Request

A store-and-forward queue (SAF) keeps the messages the other side must hear about, however long that takes, and sends them when it can. Offered nine messages, this admission check stores three:

What it stores are advices (unit 4): a reversal (0420), a completion or an offline purchase (0220), a stand-in decision (0120). Each reports something already decided, and the receiver can't refuse it, only acknowledge it. So it is safe to deliver late, and safe to deliver more than once if the receiver recognises the copies (20.6).

A request asks for a decision. Suppose a shop's 0200 at 19:20 had been queued. It goes out at 20:00 and is approved, but the terminal showed "failed" 40 minutes earlier and the customer paid cash. The card is charged for goods already paid for, and a request queued twice is two purchases. A request that can't be sent fails at once. One that was sent and got no answer is reversed, and the reversal goes into the SAF.

Timeouts aren't the only source of reversals. An approval the merchant didn't use is reversed rather than left to expire: an answer that came after the terminal gave up (unit c12 shows where QMUX puts it), a chip card that declined after the issuer approved (unit 18), a partial approval the cardholder walked away from (unit 9). Some networks also take a partial reversal, an 0420 whose field 95, replacement amounts, carries the amount that stands.

The repeat digit belongs to an attempt, not to the message (unit 4), so the queue keeps the first form and the sender sets the digit (20.3).

Entries wait for hours, on disk and in backups. What proves the card (track data, the PIN block, the card security code) is never kept (unit 8), and the check refuses such an entry instead of stripping it. A reversal with track 2 in it means the code that built it copied the whole purchase; fix that code. Chip data goes in only as far as the network asks for it (unit 18). The card number is stored unreadable, and unit 13 shows that an entry taken out of a queue can stay in the store's file.

Networks differ. Some send reversals as 0400 requests and deliver them through the SAF too, repeated as 0401. The admission check then accepts 0400 as well, and the other rules apply unchanged.

An Entry Is Stored Before Anyone Is Told, and Leaves Only When It Is Acknowledged

After a timeout the acquirer stores the reversal, records in its journal that the purchase is being reversed, and tells the terminal that the purchase failed. Does the order matter when all three take a few milliseconds? We modelled a 42.00 purchase that the host approved and whose answer was lost: the process dies after one step, and after the restart the queue is delivered.

A crash right after telling the terminal leaves the cardholder paying for a purchase the terminal showed as failed, with no reversal anywhere to undo it. A crash after storing and before telling is harmless: the terminal times out, which it also shows as a failure, and the reversal goes out after the restart. Store first, then tell.

The journal is the second trap. Written before the queue, it can say "reversal queued" when nothing is queued, and the people who reconcile trust it (unit 19). Written after, it can miss a reversal that is on its way. Put the entry and the journal record in one database transaction, so that they exist together or not at all. Stored means it survives the process and the machine: committed to disk, not held in memory or in a buffer the operating system hasn't written yet. Unit c12 keeps the queue in a persistent jPOS Space, and unit s12 shows what such a space hands back.

An entry leaves the queue only when the receiver has answered it. Taken out before it is sent, it is lost by a crash during the send (unit c12). A late 0210 that arrives while its reversal waits doesn't cancel the reversal either: the terminal has shown "failed", and the customer paid another way or left.

Keep acknowledged entries, marked as done, for as long as the journal: a dispute asks for proof that the reversal went out (unit 19).

  1. Store the reversal before the terminal hears anything

    • A queued reversal leaves only when the receiver answers it; a late approval does not withdraw it.
    • The reversal is stored, in the same transaction as its journal record, before the terminal is told the purchase failed.

Every Later Attempt Is a Repeat, and the Wait Grows Until a Reconnect Cuts It Short


The first transmission carries the stored MTI. Every later one is the repeat, 0421, with the same STAN and field 7 (unit 4). Only an attempt that went out counts: while the link is down nothing is sent, and the next attempt is still a first one. Each attempt ends, with an answer or a timeout, before the next starts, so the receiver never has two copies in flight.


How soon after a failure? A host that didn't answer is often overloaded or cut off from the issuer behind it, and then every acquirer on the network is repeating to it. Each queue that tries again every few seconds adds load when the host can least take it. The usual schedule doubles the wait after each failure, from a base up to a cap. Here the base is 30 seconds, the cap 300 and the limit eight attempts. The host never answers reversal 000318, and the link drops at 500 seconds:



A reconnect makes the head due at once, because the host may be reachable again, and networks usually expect the pending advices before new traffic. It doesn't reset the count: at 620 seconds the reversal is still a repeat, and the wait after it follows from six failures.


If a reconnect reset the count, a link that drops every few minutes would give the head fresh attempts each time. The head would never reach the limit of 20.4, and every entry behind it would wait.


After the Network's Limit an Entry Is Parked and Reported, Never Deleted


In the run, the reversal is parked after its eighth attempt, still in its stored form, and the completion behind it goes out in the same second. Networks limit how long an advice may be repeated, by a number of attempts or a time window.


Delete a parked reversal of a purchase that was posted or cleared, and the cardholder keeps paying for a purchase the terminal reported as failed. Nobody notices until the cardholder disputes it and the issuer charges it back (unit 7), with a fee for the merchant. Repeat it forever instead, and the head blocks the link: the completions behind it can miss their clearing.


A parked entry keeps its message and its history, and raises an alert with what operations needs to find the transaction: field 90, or the STAN and field 7, the amount, the last error and the masked card number. The attempt count is stored with the entry, so a restart doesn't hand out fresh attempts. What follows depends on how far the transaction has gone (unit 7). An authorization that is never cleared lets its hold lapse. A posted 0200 usually needs an adjustment through the network's exception process (unit 19).

One Sender per Link Delivers Entries in the Order They Were Decided


Each link has one queue and one sender, which takes the head and nothing else. While the head waits for its next attempt, the entries behind it wait too. Why hold up a completion because a reversal ahead of it is stuck?


Two entries can be about one transaction: a pump's completion, and its reversal after the cashier voided the sale. Here they are delivered in order, then swapped. The left column is a host that applies whatever it receives, the right one the host of 20.6:



Swapped, the reversal finds nothing to undo, and the completion that follows is posted and stays. The cardholder pays 61.30 for a sale the cashier voided. A sender can't assume that the host at the other end remembers reversals.


Two senders on one queue, as two threads or two nodes of a cluster, take the same head. The receiver gets the same reversal twice as a first transmission, and the sender records two outcomes for one entry. Give each link's queue to one node, or lock the link's entries in the shared database before sending. A lock held inside one process doesn't stop a second node.


Order is kept per link only. Two links have two queues and two schedules, so a network that is down doesn't hold up another.


The Receiver Applies Each Advice Once, by an Identity Its Repeats Share


Everything above delivers each advice at least once. When the issuer's 0230 is lost, the acquirer can't know that the completion was applied, so it sends the 0221. A crash after sending and before recording the answer sends the same entry again after the restart. Exactly-once delivery isn't possible over a link that can lose an answer, so applying each advice once is the receiver's job.


The receiver needs an identity that a repeat shares and another transaction doesn't. The identity has the layout of field 90, with the repeat digit cleared:



  • The MTI without its repeat digit: an 0221 is its 0220, while an 0220 and an 0420 are two messages.
  • The STAN wraps, so field 7 separates two uses of one STAN on different days.
  • Fields 32 and 33, because two acquirers can use the same STAN at the same moment.


Because the layout is field 90's, a reversal finds its original with one lookup. The same evening at both hosts: completion 000412 for 61.30 and its repeat, purchase 000415 for 18.00 and its reversal 000416, reversal 000418 arriving before its purchase 000417 for 25.00, and a 9.00 completion from a second acquirer, marked B, with the same STAN and field 7:



The naive host posts the repeated completion a second time, refunds the reversed purchase twice, and posts purchase 000417 after its reversal had come and gone. The other keeps two memories: the identities it posted, and the identities that reversals named. A reversal that found nothing is remembered, because its original may still come, delayed on another connection. That original is never posted: an advice is acknowledged without effect, a request is declined. Every copy gets its answer, duplicate or not, or the sender repeats it until its limit.


The identities are kept longer than the longest time a sender may go on repeating, then purged: field 7 has no year, and STANs come round. Two sessions that handle the same repeat at the same moment must not both post it. In a database, a unique constraint on the identity decides.


Networks differ. Some give every transmission a new STAN or a new field 7. The identity then uses what a repeat keeps, such as the retrieval reference number (field 37) with the terminal (field 41) and the acquirer (field 32).

Summary


  • A SAF holds reports of decisions already taken, never a request. A queued request is charged long after the terminal said "failed". The queue keeps the first form of an advice and nothing that proves the card.
  • An entry is stored before anyone is told, and leaves only when it is acknowledged. Tell first and a crash leaves an approval nobody reverses. Write the entry and the journal in one transaction, and never withdraw a reversal because a late approval came.
  • Every later attempt is a repeat, and the wait grows until a reconnect cuts it short. Fast retries load a host that is already failing; a reconnect that resets the count never lets the head reach its limit.
  • After the network's limit an entry is parked and reported, never deleted. A deleted reversal is found only as a dispute; a head repeated forever blocks the link.
  • One sender per link delivers entries in the order they were decided. A reversal that overtakes its completion leaves the completion posted at a host that doesn't remember reversals.
  • The receiver applies each advice once, by an identity its repeats share. Field 90's layout without the repeat digit finds duplicates and originals, and a remembered reversal keeps a late original from being posted.

A retry schedule: the wait after failure n is base × 2<sup>n−1</sup>, at most the cap. A reconnect makes the head due at once and keeps its count. The limit is the network's, in attempts or in time.


The receiver's identity: field 90's layout (unit 4's Reference), the fourth MTI digit made even, kept longer than the longest repeat period.

Design Note: Why Delivery Is at Least Once


A sender that gets no answer can't tell a lost message from a lost acknowledgement. It can send again and risk a duplicate, or stop and risk a loss. Money can't be lost silently, so ISO 8583 sends again, as many times as the network allows, and marks each copy with the repeat digit.


The cost lands on the receiver. It has to recognise a copy among messages that look alike for every acquirer it serves, remember what it applied for as long as any sender may still repeat it, and remember reversals for transactions it hasn't seen. The two sides are coupled through one number, the length of the repeat period, which belongs to neither of them and is set by the network's rules.


The protocol could have given each message a global ID. It identifies a message by its sender and a short counter instead (unit c12's design note), so the receiver's duplicate check is built from the same fields as field 90.
