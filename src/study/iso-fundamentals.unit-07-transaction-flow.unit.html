The ATM took the money at once; the hotel took less than it asked for, three days later. The grocer's purchase was approved while the bank's own host was down. Who decided each line, and which message moved the money? Five rules, one per section heading.

A Payment Crosses Four Parties, and Each Hop Is a Link of Its Own

The card is read at a merchant's terminal. The terminal's request goes to the acquirer, the merchant's bank or a processor working for it. The acquirer passes it to a card network (Visa, Mastercard, a domestic scheme), which picks the issuer, the cardholder's bank, from the card number's leading digits. The issuer approves or declines, and the answer goes back the same way.

The issuer approves or declines the purchase; the acquirer and the network can decline it on their own checks, and approve it only in the cases of 7.4. Each hop is a separate ISO 8583 link with its own specification: its own packager, header and private fields (units 1 and 5). Every hop rebuilds the message and passes on only the fields its specification defines. An acquirer that puts a loyalty tag for the issuer into field 48 sees it arrive nowhere unless the network's specification carries that tag. The issuer decides without it, often with no error on either side.

Each hop also waits for the next. The network gives the issuer a few seconds; when the issuer is down or slow, the network may decide in its place (7.4). The acquirer's own timeout has to cover the whole path.

An Approval Holds the Money in the Dual-Message Flow and Moves It in the Single-Message Flow

Unit 4 showed that an approved 0100 places a hold and an approved 0200 posts the debit. An issuer keeps two numbers for an account: the ledger, the money posted, and what is available, the ledger minus open holds. A model of the opening's account, starting at 500.00:

The hotel's 0100, the ATM's 0200, and Thursday's clearing record for the hotel:

In the dual-message flow the 0100 only asks for permission. The issuer holds 300.00 and returns an approval code in field 38. No money has moved. Days later the acquirer sends the clearing record, usually in a batch file to the network rather than as an online message. It carries the final amount and the approval code that ties it to the 0110. The issuer posts 260.00 and releases the whole hold (unit 19 takes clearing apart). Credit card purchases mostly work this way, and it suits merchants whose amount isn't final at the counter: hotels, car rentals, fuel and tips. On some links an advice carries the final amount online first, as the fuel pump's completion did in unit 4; the money still moves with clearing.

In the single-message flow the 0200 carries everything needed to authorize and to post, and the issuer posts at once. There is no clearing record per transaction. ATM withdrawals and PIN debit purchases usually work this way.

When an approval is taken for a payment:

  • The merchant never sends the clearing. A terminal whose batch is never closed has approvals and nothing else. After some days, set by the issuer and the network's rules, the issuer drops the uncleared hold. The cardholder has the goods, and a merchant that presents the sales late risks having them charged back as unauthorized.
  • The acquirer clears the authorized amount. The hotel's guest is charged 300.00 for a 260.00 stay, and gets the 40.00 back only by a refund or a dispute (7.5).

In the single-message flow the online message is the financial record. An approved 0200 whose answer is lost is a debit already posted, which is why unit 4 reverses it at once.

Settlement Moves One Net Amount per Institution, Days After the Purchases

Clearing says who owes whom; settlement pays it. The network adds up each member's cleared transactions for the settlement day and instructs the settlement banks to move one net amount per member. Three banks, each both an acquirer and an issuer, with eight cleared purchases between them (fees left out):

Each purchase is owed to its acquirer and owed by its issuer. Eight purchases worth 970.50 become three transfers, and 194.50 moves. The acquirer then pays each merchant its day's sales, less a service charge that includes the interchange fee the issuer keeps out of each purchase.

A merchant who checks each sale against the bank's deposits matches nothing. What can be checked is totals: the online 05xx reconciliation messages of the 1987 edition let the two ends of a link agree on counts and amounts, and move no money themselves (unit 19).

A Decision Taken Elsewhere Arrives as an Advice, and the Issuer Must Apply It

Two parties may approve a purchase without asking the issuer. When the issuer doesn't answer in time, the network may stand in: it decides within limits the issuer agreed beforehand, then reports each decision with an authorization advice, 0120. And a terminal may approve a small purchase offline, below its floor limit; that one reaches the issuer as a clearing record, or as a 0220 advice on links that use them. Unit 4 showed that an advice is acknowledged, never declined.

The grocer's 150.00 from the opening was a stand-in approval, reported at 12:05 when the issuer's host was back. After the ATM withdrawal, 150.00 is available. The issuer's handler for stand-in advices:

  1. Coding practice

    Keep the account right after decisions you did not make

    • A stand-in or offline approval reaches the issuer as an advice or a clearing record, and the issuer applies it as if it had approved it, even when the account goes below zero.
    • In the dual-message flow an approval only holds the money; the clearing record posts the final amount and releases the whole hold, and without it the hold lapses and the merchant is never paid.

What Undoes a Transaction Depends on How Far It Has Gone


The purchase isUndo it withSent byWhere
approved, but the answer was lost or the terminal could not finisha reversal, 0420 (unit 4)acquirerthe online link, at once
approved and cancelled by the cashier the same day, a voida reversal, usuallyacquirerthe online link, before clearing
cleared, and the goods are returneda refund, a new credit with its own STAN (unit 5)merchant, through the acquirera new transaction
cleared, and the cardholder disputes ita chargebackissuerthe network's dispute process, in clearing


A reversal of an 0100 releases the hold; a reversal of an 0200 gives the money back. Either works only within a window the network's rules set: for an 0100, typically before it is cleared, for an 0200, typically the same business day. Some terminal protocols carry a void as a processing code of its own, and the acquirer's host turns it into a reversal on the network link.


Suppose a merchant's system answers every return with an 0420, as it does for voids. A customer brings goods back a week after the purchase cleared. The reversal is past the window: the network declines it or matches it to nothing, depending on its rules. The cleared purchase stands. The merchant's records say "reversed", while the cardholder still sees the charge. The cardholder disputes it, and the issuer charges it back. The merchant gives back the sale as it meant to, and pays a chargeback fee on top.


A dispute runs in stages: the issuer's chargeback with a reason code, the acquirer's representment with the merchant's evidence, and, when they still disagree, a decision by the network. All of it happens in clearing files and the networks' dispute systems, not on the online link. Each network sets its own stages and reason codes.


Editions differ. The 1993 edition defines chargeback messages in its 14xx class, and Mastercard's clearing files use 1442 for a chargeback. The 1987 edition's 04xx class defines reversals only.

Summary


  • A payment crosses four parties, and each hop is a link of its own. The issuer decides, except in stand-in, and only what the next link's specification defines gets across.
  • An approval holds the money in the dual-message flow and moves it in the single-message flow. The clearing record carries the final amount and releases the hold. Without it the hold lapses and nothing is paid; with the authorized amount instead of the final one, the cardholder is overcharged.
  • Settlement moves one net amount per institution, days later. No purchase appears in a transfer of its own; totals are what can be reconciled.
  • A decision taken elsewhere arrives as an advice, and the issuer must apply it. An advice that is acknowledged and not recorded leaves available too high, and the next approval overdraws the account.
  • What undoes a transaction depends on how far it has gone: a reversal while it is open, a refund or a chargeback once it has cleared.

Challenges


  1. A restaurant takes an 0100 for the bill and the customer adds a 15% tip afterwards. What amount goes into clearing, and what should the issuer do when it is above the held amount?
  2. Your issuer stands in for itself: while the core banking system is down, the authorization host approves up to 50.00 per purchase from a copy of each balance taken at midnight. What must happen when the core comes back, and how much can one card overspend?
  3. In the netting of 7.3, Bank A fails to pay its 93.00. Who is short, by how much, and what could the network have required of its members beforehand?


Design Note: Why Two Flows?


The dual-message flow lets the amount be fixed after the decision. A hotel, a fuel pump or a restaurant knows the final amount only later, so the online message asks for permission and a cheaper batch carries the final amount. The cost is two records per purchase that have to be matched, holds that lapse when the second record never comes, and final amounts that differ from what was approved.


The single-message flow suits the counter where the amount is final: an ATM, a PIN debit purchase. One message decides and posts, and nothing waits for a batch. Here the price is paid on the online link: every message on it is money, so a lost answer is a posted debit, and the reversals and advices that repair it have to reach the issuer however long that takes (unit 20).
