A gateway sits between web shops that call its REST API with JSON and an acquirer's ISO 8583 link. It also forwards some terminals' purchases to a third-party gateway that speaks only REST. Its first month in production brought four tickets:

TicketWhat happened
1A Tokyo shop's terminal sold an item for 2500 yen, and the third-party gateway charged 25 yen
2An advice for a purchase on 30 December, delivered on 2 January, was dated 30 December of the new year
3A debit terminal refunded 25.00, and the cardholder was charged 25.00
4A web shop's HTTP client timed out, retried, and the customer paid twice

Every mapping had passed its tests. The fixes come down to five rules, one per section heading.

Each Endpoint Maps Into One Internal Format and Out of It

Every message that enters the gateway is mapped into one internal format first. The business logic works only on that format, and each destination maps out of it. Here the internal format is a record of full values:

A refund arrives from a 1987 terminal as fields 3, 4, 12, 13, 41 and 49 (200000, 000000002500, 183000, 1230, 29110001, 840), mapped two seconds later on a UTC clock. The same refund arrives from a web shop as JSON ("refund", "25.00", "USD", "2026-12-30T18:30:00", "29110001"). Each goes through its own mapping:

The two wire forms can't be compared with each other, and every rule the gateway applies (limits, routing, the duplicate check of 12.5) would otherwise have to read both. In the record, the decisions of 12.2 to 12.4 have been taken: where the decimal point goes, which year it is, which kind of transaction.

Suppose the gateway has four kinds of source (1987 terminals, the REST API, a 1993 network link, a batch file) and three destinations. Mapped pair by pair, that is twelve mappings, each deciding amounts, dates and codes for itself. A fix for ticket 1 has to be found and repeated in every mapping that carries yen, and the one nobody remembers keeps charging a hundredth of the price. With an internal format there are seven mappings, one per endpoint, and a new endpoint adds one.

jPOS publishes its own internal format, the Common Message Format (CMF), based on the 2003 edition and released under a Creative Commons licence; 2.1.7 ships its packager as jar:packager/cmf.xml. Whether you adopt it or write your own, packagers and channels only move bytes, and the integration work is in the mappings, whether the other side speaks JSON, XML or SOAP.

CMF version 3, an early-access draft at the time of writing, follows the dataset model of ISO 8583:2023 (unit 17).

An Amount Is Minor Units, and the Currency Says Where the Point Goes

Field 4 holds the amount in the currency's smallest unit, with no decimal point. The currency code in field 49 says how many of those digits are decimals: two for US dollars, none for yen, three for Bahraini dinars. jPOS's ISOCurrency knows the exponent of each currency:

Ticket 1 is the middle line: the original mapping divided every amount by 100. It also charges a dinar purchase ten times its price.

In the other direction, a JSON amount becomes field 4. Read into a double, it loses cents:

The double nearest to 19.99 is a little less than 19.99, so 100 times it is a little less than 1999, and the cast to long drops the fraction. Keep the amount a BigDecimal from the JSON parser to the field. ISOCurrency.toISO87String then refuses an amount with more decimals than the currency has: 19.99 yen is an error, not 1999 yen or 20.

ISOCurrency takes most of its table from the JDK, which also lists codes that are not money. Field 49 = 999 means "no currency", with an exponent of -1:

So the currencies a gateway accepts are its own list, checked before the amount is read (12.4).

A Date Without a Year Takes the Year Nearest to the Clock You Pass In

Field 13, the local transaction date, is MMDD, and field 12 is hhmmss. Field 7, the transmission time, has no year either. A REST API wants a full date, so the mapping has to choose the year. Three ways to choose it, applied to two messages from a terminal on UTC:

The first message is ticket 2: an advice that waited in the terminal's store-and-forward queue (unit 20) since 30 December. The year of the clock dates it 362 days in the future. A third-party gateway that refuses future dates refuses the advice, and the terminal repeats it until someone fixes the year. A gateway that accepts it books the purchase in the wrong year, where reconciliation (unit 19) can't find it.

"A date still ahead of now is last year" fixes the first message and keeps the second wrong. The terminal's clock runs seven seconds ahead, so it stamps a purchase 00:00:05 on 1 January while the gateway's clock still says 31 December. The year of the clock puts it in the past year, and the rule, seeing a date seven seconds ahead, moves it there too.

ISODate.parseISODate(mmddhhmmss, nowMillis, timeZone) tries the previous, the current and the next year, and keeps the one nearest to now. It is wrong only for a date more than six months away, which an online message doesn't carry. Fields 12 and 13 are the terminal's local time, so compare in the terminal's time zone.

Pass the clock in instead of reading it inside the mapping, so a test can map a message at 23:59:58 on 31 December.

A Value With No Entry in the Table Is Refused, Never Guessed

Codes map through explicit tables: the processing code, the currency, the third-party gateway's status. A value a table lacks is refused. Here are the first version and the table that replaced it, on five processing codes:

Field 3 has three parts: two digits for the transaction type, two for the account the money comes from, two for the account it goes to. A debit terminal that names the cardholder's cheque account sends its refund as 200020. The first version compared all six digits, found no match, and sent a sale. That is ticket 3. The cardholder was charged 25.00 instead of credited, a difference of 50.00, while the merchant's report says "refunded". The cash advance and the balance inquiry went out as sales too.

The table keys on the transaction type and refuses the rest. The terminal gets a decline (12, invalid transaction), and the cash advance waits until someone adds an entry for it.

The reply comes back through a table too. The third-party gateway's statuses map to field 39 through a table built from both specifications, as unit 9 does for response codes. A status the table lacks gets 96, and the payment is cancelled at the gateway, since it may have been an approval. Copy none of the gateway's error text into the response: free-text fields such as 44 reach the terminal and the acquirer's logs, and the text can hold a stack trace, an SQL error or a card number.

Problem statement

Your switch now takes purchases from a bank's terminals in Germany, the United Kingdom, Switzerland, Chile and Kuwait, and forwards them to a REST gateway as POST /charges. Finance has found charges that don't match what cardholders signed for, and the gateway's operator has sent back requests it says should never have reached it.

ChargeMapper.toCharge in src/main/java/com/example/practice/ChargeMapper.java builds each Charge from a terminal's 1987 request. It is written except for three methods marked TODO:

  • kind: the gateway knows two kinds, "sale" and "refund";
  • amount: the amount as the gateway reads it, a decimal number in the request's currency. The currencies in the gateway's contract are in ACCEPTED;
  • localTime: the terminal's local date and time of the purchase, as the gateway's reports and date checks will read it.

toCharge is called with the moment of mapping and the terminal's time zone, so that the tests can map requests at any moment of the year. A request the gateway can't take is refused with an ISOException and never sent. About ten lines in total.

A Retried Call Carries the Same Idempotency Key, So It Is Applied Once


A web shop's call to POST /payments times out. The shop can't tell whether the payment happened, just like the ISO sender in unit 4. The usual answer in REST payment APIs is the idempotency key: the shop makes a unique value for each payment, sends it in a header such as Idempotency-Key, and sends the same value when it retries. The gateway applies the first call with a key and answers every later one with the stored outcome:



In each run the host approves the 0200 for 25.00, the answer to the shop is lost, and the shop retries:



Without a key, the retry is a second purchase. That is ticket 4. With the key, the retry gets the first call's approval code, and the host saw one purchase.


Unit 4 forbade resending an 0200 because an ISO host makes no promise about a second copy. With a key, the receiver makes that promise: it remembers each key and what came of it. So the shop may retry, and the gateway behind it still never resends the 0200.


(1) Claim the key atomically. If two retries arrive at the same moment, and each looks for the key before either saves it, both go to the host:



An Entry starts with the status IN_PROGRESS, so a retry that finds the key while the first call still waits gets that answer and tries again later. In a database, a unique constraint on the key column makes the claim.


(2) Same key, another payment: refused. A shop that reuses a key by mistake would otherwise get the first payment's approval for a payment that was never sent.


(3) The stored outcome, whatever it was. When the host doesn't answer at step (4), the gateway reverses the 0200 (unit 4) and stores "failed" under the key. A retry gets "failed", not a second 0200. To try again, the shop sends a new payment with a new key.


Store the key with what the network knows the transaction by: the STAN (field 11), the retrieval reference number (field 37), the terminal and the time. A reversal, a refund or a dispute will need them.


When your switch is the REST client, for the terminals behind the third-party gateway, the same rule applies from the other side. Send a key with each payment, keep it with the ISO request, and after a timeout repeat the call with the same key: the stored outcome tells you what happened. A gateway API with no idempotency key and no way to cancel a payment leaves no safe move after a timeout. Check for both before you integrate with it. The gateway's POST /payments now keeps its idempotency keys in a database table instead of a map in memory, so that they survive a restart and every gateway node sees the same keys. Shops' HTTP clients give up after a few seconds and call again with the same Idempotency-Key. Some call twice at once from two servers, and one shop's release reused keys for a day.


Write pay in src/main/java/com/example/practice/PaymentsEndpoint.java. A cardholder pays at most once for each payment a shop sends, and every call, first or repeated, tells the shop the truth about that payment with one of the statuses listed on Result.


Everything else is in the file. KeyTable behaves like a table with a unique constraint on its key column. Summary


  • Each endpoint maps into one internal format and out of it. Decisions about amounts, dates and codes are taken once per endpoint. Pairwise mappings repeat them, and a fix misses one path.
  • An amount is minor units, and the currency says where the point goes. Two decimals assumed turns 2500 yen into 25; a double loses cents; 999 has an exponent of -1.
  • A date without a year takes the year nearest to the clock you pass in. The year of the clock dates a delayed advice a year ahead, and "ahead means last year" dates a fast terminal's message a year back.
  • A value with no entry in the table is refused. A default branch turned a refund into a charge. An unknown reply status gets 96 and a cancellation, and the gateway's error text stays out of the response.
  • A retried call carries the same idempotency key. The gateway claims each key atomically, refuses it for another payment and answers retries with the stored outcome, failures included. Toward the host it still sends one 0200 per key.

Challenges


  1. Your gateway keeps idempotency keys for 24 hours. A shop's nightly job retries every failed call from the past week with its original key. What does the job get on the second night, and what should the gateway answer?
  2. The business wants cash-back purchases (09 in field 3) next month. What has to change in the internal format, in the table of 12.4, and on the third-party gateway's side?
  3. Two of the shop's servers, behind a load balancer, send the same key 50 ms apart. Trace both calls through a gateway whose keys live in a database with a unique constraint, including the moment the second call arrives.
  4. Field 7 is in UTC, and field 13 is the terminal's local date. For a terminal in Auckland, when do the two disagree about the day, and which one should the internal format keep?


Design Note: Why REST Retries and ISO 8583 Reverses


Both protocols face a sender that got no answer and doesn't know whether the money moved. They keep the record that settles it on different sides.


ISO 8583 puts it on the sender. A host promises nothing about a second copy of a request, so the sender undoes instead of asking again. It has to keep each request until it knows the outcome, build a reversal that points back through field 90, and keep that reversal in a queue until the host acknowledges it (units 4 and 20). The host stays simple, and every sender carries the machinery.


REST with idempotency keys puts it on the receiver. The receiver keeps every key and its outcome for as long as retries may come, under a unique constraint, so a sender only needs to remember the key. The receiver's key store grows with its traffic.


A gateway between the two carries both. It keeps keys for the shops in front and reverses toward the acquirer behind, and it stores one record per payment: the key, the outcome, and the STAN the reversal will need.
