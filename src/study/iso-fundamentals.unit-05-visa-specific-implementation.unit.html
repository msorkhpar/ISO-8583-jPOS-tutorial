A processor has run a binary link to a domestic switch for years, and now it connects to Visa's test system. Its reader expects the MTI right after a four-byte length, as the domestic switch sends it. It takes the first frame from Visa for an MTI 1601 and gives up on field 35, which the message doesn't carry.

Visa's interface specifications are confidential, available to Visa's clients and to the processors that connect for them. This unit uses only public information and what jPOS 2.1.7 ships for Visa links, and the outputs come from jPOS 2.1.7. Four rules change on a Visa link, one per section heading.

On a BASE I Link, a Header Sits Between the Length and the MTI

BASE I, Visa's authorization service, puts a four-byte length prefix and a 22-byte header in front of every message. jPOS's VAPChannel writes that frame and Base1Packager writes the message. This program sends one 0100 to a socket on 127.0.0.1 and captures the bytes:

The PAN's length differs from unit 3's BCD prefix: Base1Packager writes sixteen as the binary byte 10.

The destination and source are station IDs that Visa assigns. A VAPChannel configured from a descriptor swaps them when it sends back a message it received.

The domestic reader from the opening takes the MTI from the byte after the length. Here that byte is the header's length, so it reads MTI 1601, takes the rest of the header for a bitmap, and fails on a field 35 that isn't there.

The network also sends frames with a length of zero, which jPOS calls polls. VAPChannel writes each one back and keeps reading (the run in 5.2 shows it). A reader that treats a zero length as an error drops a healthy link; one that treats it as a message hands an empty message to the application.

A Rejected Message Comes Back Behind a Longer Header, With No Field 39

An issuer that refuses a purchase answers it: an 0110 with a response code in field 39 (unit 9). Visa's network can also refuse a message before any issuer sees it, for instance a malformed field. jPOS models that as a reject: the network sends your message back behind a 26-byte reject header, in front of the original 22-byte one. The reject header's format byte has its top bit set (another header follows), and its last four bytes hold a bitmap and a reject code. Built from the 5.1 frame, with a made-up code:

Your own 0100 comes back, with your STAN and no field 39. The refusal shows only in the 48 bytes of header, which (BASE1Header) m.getISOHeader() returns: isRejected() and the reject code, whose meaning the specification lists.

A client that waits for the answer to its STAN and checks field 39 finds nothing there and reports "declined". The cardholder tries another card, the same broken field is rejected again, and the log shows a run of declines while no issuer has seen a single purchase. Check the header for a reject before you look for field 39, and log the reject code: it points at the field to fix.

Your specification decides. The layout here follows jPOS's BASE1Header and VAPChannel. Take the reject header, its codes and what the network returns behind it from your specification.

The MTI and the Processing Code Together Name the Transaction


Visa runs two kinds of processing on one network. In the dual-message style, an authorization (0100) goes online to BASE I, and the financial details follow later in a clearing batch through BASE II. In the single-message style, Visa's Single Message System (SMS) takes one 0200 that authorizes and clears together, which is usual for ATMs and PIN debit (unit 7 follows both flows).


Field 3, the processing code, says what kind of transaction it is. Its six digits are two for the type, two for the account the money comes from and two for the account it goes to, 00 when unspecified. The types you meet first:


Field 3, first two digitsTransaction
00purchase of goods or services
01cash withdrawal or cash advance
20refund, a credit to the cardholder
30, 31funds or balance inquiry, depending on the network


A gateway's daily report sees four messages from terminal 29110001. Which of them are purchases?


MTIField 3Amount
010000000042.00
020000300018.50
042000000030.00
020020000018.50


The first two. Their class digits say authorization and financial, and 003000 differs from 000000 only in the from-account digits. The third is a reversal of an earlier purchase: class 4 says it undoes an earlier message, and its field 3 repeats what that message was. The fourth is a refund.


A report that reads field 3 alone counts the reversal as a third purchase: 30.00 of sales that the merchant has given back. One that compares the whole field with 000000 misses the second purchase.


A refund is a new transaction, not an undo. A merchant who takes back two of five items a week after the sale sends a refund for those two, with its own amount and STAN. A reversal points back at a message whose outcome is still open (unit 4), and a week after the sale the purchase has been cleared. Cancelling a sale before it is cleared, a void, usually goes to the network as a reversal (unit 7).

A Private Field Can Hold a Message of Its Own, Laid Out by This Release's Specification


Most fields keep their ISO 8583 meaning on Visa. Visa's own data goes into the private-use fields, and some of those hold a message with its own bitmap. In jPOS's Base1Packager, fields 126 and 127 are such fields:




Field 126 starts after field 11's 000002: a length byte 0E (14), an 8-byte bitmap of its own with bit 5 set, and sub-field 5 in EBCDIC. The same six characters set as plain text pack without a complaint, as 06 and six ASCII bytes. The receiver reads field 126 as a bitmap and sub-fields, finds six bytes where the bitmap needs eight, and fails with an error that names no field. A test that only packs the message passes; one that also unpacks it fails.


These layouts change: Visa, like Mastercard, publishes mandated message changes on a regular schedule, usually twice a year. Base1Packager names its field 126 sub-fields after an older layout, such as "Cardholder Cert Serial Number", and leaves fields 62 and 63 as plain bytes for you to split. Write the packager for your link from the current specification and test it against the network's sample messages. Keep the mapping between your own message and Visa's in one component, so a release changes that component and not every application behind it.

Summary


  • On a BASE I link, a header sits between the length and the MTI. The length counts the header too, a zero length is a poll to answer, and a reader that expects the MTI after the length misreads the whole message.
  • A rejected message comes back behind a longer header, with no field 39. Check the header before field 39, or a network reject is reported as a decline and its cause is lost.
  • The MTI and the processing code together name the transaction. The class digit says authorization, financial or reversal, field 3's first two digits say purchase, cash or refund, and the account digits change neither. A refund is a new credit, not a reversal.
  • A private field can hold a message of its own, laid out by this release's specification. A sub-message set as text packs and fails at the receiver, and a layout copied once is wrong after the next release.

Challenges


  1. A capture from a Visa link holds a frame whose prefix says 005A and whose header starts 1A81. Where does the message start, and what would you look for in the header before you read field 39?
  2. Your client handles polls correctly, and the link carries no traffic between 02:00 and 06:00. What would you want to know from the specification about who polls whom, and what an unanswered poll costs?
  3. A refund arrives for a card your system has never seen, because the purchase went through another acquirer. Which fields would you check before you credit the cardholder, and which would you need from the network?
  4. Next spring's release adds a sub-field to field 126. List every place in your code that has to change, then design the code so that the list has one entry.


Design Note: Why a Bitmap Inside a Field?


ISO 8583:1987 has 128 fields, and a network that wants to carry more data has two choices: give new meaning to fields it doesn't use, or nest. Field 126 nests: inside one private field it has a bitmap and a table of sub-fields, the same scheme as the message around it.


Nesting lets a network add data in a release without a new field number. A sub-field that a message doesn't carry costs one bit, and a receiver that has the new table reads the new sub-field the way it reads any other.


The cost is the one unit 1 described for the whole message: a reader can't skip what its table doesn't describe. Without the current table nobody can read past an unknown sub-field, and a sender and a receiver on different releases misread each other's field 126 while the rest of the message is fine.
