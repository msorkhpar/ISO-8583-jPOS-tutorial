A Clearing Record Carries the Final Amount and the Approval It Completes

In the dual-message flow an approval moves no money (unit 7). After the sale the acquirer sends one clearing record, a presentment, per transaction, usually in a file at the end of the merchant's day. The issuer posts it and releases the hold the approval placed. To do the second, it has to know which approval the record completes.

A restaurant authorizes a dinner at 120.00, and the guest adds a tip, so 138.00 is presented. The acquirer builds the record from its journal: the request, the response and the final amount. The issuer is unit 7's account model, which posts a clearing record and releases the hold whose approval code the record carries:

We run the dinner twice. In the first run the clearing system copies field 38 from the journal; in the second it rebuilds the records from the terminal's batch file, which has no approval code:

Without field 38 the issuer posts 138.00 and can't tell which hold it completes, so the 120.00 hold stays until it lapses, days later by the network's rules. Until then the cardholder can spend 120.00 less than the account holds, and the next morning's purchase of 300.00 is declined. A presentment the issuer can't tie to an approval may also be charged back as unauthorized, and the acquirer passes that loss to the merchant.

So the record carries, besides the card, the amount, the date and the merchant:

  • the approval code (DE 38) and the network's own trace identifier from the authorization response, which tie it to the approval;
  • the terminal and the retrieval reference number (DE 41 and 37), by which the acquirer finds the sale in its own records (19.5);
  • for a chip sale, the chip data (DE 55) with the card's cryptogram (unit 18), which for an offline approval is the issuer's only proof that the card approved;
  • an acquirer reference number (ARN), which the acquirer assigns when it clears the sale and which every later dispute quotes. In 1993-based layouts it travels in DE 31, acquirer reference data.

The presented amount may differ from the authorized one: the tip of unit 14, the hotel's minibar, the fuel pump that authorized 100.00 and delivered 61.40. Each network sets how far the two may differ before a new authorization is needed.

Clearing records travel in files on the network's schedule, not on the authorization link, and the network sends files back: acknowledgements, rejected records, chargebacks and settlement reports. The files carry full card numbers, so they belong to the cardholder data environment (unit 13). jPOS 2.1.7 has no clearing component. A jPOS application keeps the journal the records are built from, and a DirPoll (unit s17) can pick up the files that come back.

Networks differ. The file layouts are the networks'. Some are built from 1993-edition messages, as Mastercard's are (unit 7); others use fixed-length records of their own.

On a single-message link there is no clearing record: the approved 0200 posts at once and is the record the network settles from (unit 7). Every 0200, advice and reversal on that link changes what the acquirer is paid, and the totals of 19.3 are the first check before the money moves.

A Transaction Belongs to the Business Day That the Network's Cutover Gives It

A network's business day ends at its cutover, a time of day in the network's own time zone. A transaction after it belongs to the next business day, and weekends and the network's holidays roll forward to the next day it settles. That day is the settlement date, DE 15: MMDD in the 1987 edition, YYMMDD in the 1993 one.

The opening's acquirer is on a network whose cutover is 18:00 in New York. One of its purchases, for 18.50, was made on Friday 6 November at 18:00:30 New York time. Its reconciliation dated transactions by its servers' UTC calendar date, with weekends rolled to Monday. Here are five instants through that calendar, through an 18:00 cutover at a fixed UTC-5, and through the network's own calendar, with Thanksgiving, 26 November, as a holiday:

The server's calendar put the 18.50 on Friday, and the network put it on Monday. Friday's totals at the two ends differ by 18.50, and Monday's by 18.50 the other way. Neither day reconciles, although both ends hold every message. The same calendar puts Wednesday's purchases between 18:00 and 19:00 on Wednesday, when the network, closed on Thursday, settles them on Friday.

The fixed offset is right all winter. From March to November New York is UTC-4, so a fixed UTC-5 puts every purchase between 18:00 and 19:00 local time on the day before (the last line). Both ends have to apply the same three details:

  • the zone is the network's, with its daylight saving time, not the server's or a fixed offset;
  • the cutover moment itself belongs to the next day (here) or to the day before, as the network writes it down;
  • the holidays are the network's, which need not be the country's.

Often the host sets DE 15 in its response, since only the host knows which side of its cutover the request arrived on. The acquirer records that value and counts by it, and computes the date itself only where no host has answered yet, as for an advice in its store-and-forward queue (unit 20). DE 15 in the 1987 layout has no year, so take the year nearest to the transaction, as unit 12 does for field 13.

A message that crosses the cutover counts on its own day. An 0420 queued at 17:59 for one of Friday's purchases and delivered at 18:02 carries Monday's DE 15. Both ends count the purchase on Friday and the reversal on Monday, so reconciliation compares days, not transactions. Some networks have their own rules for late reversals; follow them at both ends.

Both Ends Count the Day by the Same Rules, and a Reversal Is Counted in Fields of Its Own


After the cutover the two ends of a link can compare what they counted, with an 0500 reconciliation request and its 0510 answer. The request carries counts and amounts per category: credits and debits, each with its reversals, and authorizations (Reference). "Debit" and "credit" are from the cardholder's side: a purchase debits the account, a refund credits it.


The acquirer counts from its journal. Each entry is a request with the DE 39 and DE 15 of its answer; reversals are written once acknowledged and carry no DE 39. One acquirer's journal for Friday, with DE 90 shortened:



Four rules decide which entries count, and where:


  • only the entries whose DE 15 is the day being reconciled, so the last purchase waits for Monday;
  • only approved requests and acknowledged reversals, since the declined 99.00 moved nothing;
  • an approved 0100 counts in DE 81 and nowhere else, because it moved no money;
  • a reversal counts once, in the reversal fields, and the debit it reverses stays counted. The 0420 and its repeat 0421 name the same original in DE 90, so they are one reversal:



The day counted by those rules, and by two counters that each break one of them:



The counter that subtracts the reversal from the debits arrives at the same net, C8500, with four other fields different. The receiver answers it "out of balance" (19.4), and both ends spend the morning on a day where no money differs. The separate fields are there to say where two ends disagree: a missing debit is a purchase one side never saw, a missing debit reversal is a reversal that never arrived.


The counter that counts the 0421 again lowers the net by 25.00, as if the purchase had been reversed twice. Repeats are normal on any link, because an advice is repeated until it is acknowledged (units 4 and 20).


Sum amounts as long minor units of their own currency (unit 11); a link that settles in several currencies keeps one set of totals per currency.

The Receiver Compares Every Total, Because Equal Nets Can Hide Different Counts


The acquirer sends its totals for the day named in DE 15. The receiver counts the same day from its own records and answers with a settlement code in DE 66: 1 in balance, 2 out of balance, 3 error (the usual 1987 values; each network publishes its own list). The comparison, and the sign of DE 97:



The 0500 for 19.3's day, packed with jPOS's iso87ascii.xml, and the answers of three receivers:



The first receiver never got the reversal, so its DE 77, 89 and 97 differ. The second saw every message and subtracted the reversal. Its net equals the acquirer's, and only the counts show that the two ends counted differently. A receiver that compared DE 97 alone would answer 1, and the two ends would go on counting by different rules.


DE 97 is a sign followed by 16 digits, and jPOS packs it with IFA_AMOUNT, which expects that form. Which party C and D refer to is each network's definition; here C means the sender of the totals is owed the net.


What does a 2 change? No money moves with an 0510, and neither side's totals are corrected. Settlement pays by the network's records, and the 2 tells both ends to find the transactions behind the difference (19.5). It is no reason to reverse anything, since a reversal is for a request whose outcome is unknown (unit 4), and no reason to send the 0500 again: it has been answered, and a recount gives the same totals until a record changes. Only an 0500 that gets no answer is sent again, as the network specifies, often as an 0501 repeat. It asks about records and moves no money, so unit 4's rule that an unanswered request is reversed doesn't apply.


Editions differ. The 1993 edition uses 1500 to 1530. DE 24, the function code, tells a final reconciliation from a checkpoint during the day, DE 28 is the reconciliation date and DE 29 an indicator, and the verdict is an action code in DE 39. DE 66 holds original fees there.


Networks differ. Many networks never send 05xx online and reconcile only through settlement reports in files. The counting rules of 19.3 apply to those reports too.

Matching Puts Every Record in One Category, and Each Category Has an Owner


Totals say that Friday is out of balance, not which transactions. To find them, match records one by one: the approvals against the presentments, the presentments against the network's acknowledgements, and on a single-message link the journal against the network's settlement report. Unit 15 matches every day, even when the totals agree, because differences that offset each other vanish from a total.


A match needs a key that both records carry. For the acquirer that is the terminal (DE 41) with the RRN (DE 37), because an RRN is unique per terminal, not across the network. DE 38 ties a presentment to its approval, DE 90 a reversal to its original, and the ARN a dispute to its presentment. Approvals from one merchant's terminals against the clearing file, with the last three digits of each RRN:




Keyed on the RRN alone, the two sales numbered 101 share one key. Run that way, terminal 29110001's approval of 100.00 vanished from the report, terminal 29110002's 36.00 was paired with a presentment of 100.00, and its own presentment of 41.40 came out as a duplicate.


Each category has an owner:


  • Amount differs. A tip within the network's tolerance is normal. Beyond it the presentment may be refused or charged back.
  • Duplicate presentment. The same sale submitted twice charges the cardholder twice. The acquirer has to catch it before the file leaves; otherwise the cardholder disputes it and it returns as a chargeback.
  • Not authorized. A presentment with no approval on record: an offline sale below the floor limit, an error, or fraud. Without an approval the acquirer has nothing to answer a chargeback with.
  • Not presented. An approval the merchant never cleared. The other 42.00 of the opening's 60.50 is one: terminal 29110001 approved it and its batch was never closed. The merchant isn't paid, and the cardholder's hold lapses by itself.


Disputes arrive the same way, as new records on a later day. A chargeback quotes the ARN, carries a reason code, and debits the acquirer in the clearing and settlement of the day it arrives, not of the sale (unit 7 gives the stages). Several common reasons are this section's categories, found by the issuer instead of the acquirer: duplicate processing, no authorization, an amount other than the one approved. The acquirer answers from its journal: the approval code, the RRN, the STAN and transmission time, and proof that a reversal was sent when an outcome was unknown. A reversal that never got through, parked by its store-and-forward queue (unit 20), is settled the same way: the acquirer usually raises an adjustment through the network's exception process, another new record on a later day.

Summary


  • A clearing record carries the final amount and the approval it completes. Without DE 38 and the network's trace identifier the issuer posts the sale and keeps the hold, and the presentment can be charged back as unauthorized.
  • A transaction belongs to the business day the network's cutover gives it: in the network's zone with its daylight saving time, with the cutover moment counted as the network writes it, weekends and network holidays rolled forward, and the host's DE 15 when it sends one. A server's calendar day puts purchases near the cutover on another day, and both days fail to reconcile.
  • Both ends count by the same rules: that day's DE 15 only, approved requests and acknowledged reversals only, authorizations outside the money fields, and each reversal once, in fields of its own.
  • The receiver compares every total, because equal nets can hide different counts. A 2 in DE 66 moves no money; it starts the search for the transactions.
  • Matching puts every record in one category on a key both sides carry, the terminal with the RRN. Every category but "matched" has an owner, and what the acquirer misses comes back as a chargeback.

Challenges


  1. Your network moves its cutover from 18:00 to 17:00 New York time from next Monday. Which days' totals does the change touch, what do you deploy when, and how do you test it before Monday?
  2. Your 0510 for Friday says 2, and the difference is one debit of 18.50 and nothing else. Which mistakes of 19.2 and which categories of 19.5 could explain it, and which records do you pull first?
  3. A terminal resets its RRN counter after maintenance and reuses 631010000101 in the same week. What happens in the matcher of 19.5, and which field would you add to the key?
  4. A merchant asks why a sale approved at 120.00 shows as 138.00 on the statement and why, for three days, the cardholder's available balance was 120.00 lower. Write the answer from the records the acquirer holds.


Design Note: Why Totals Before Transactions?


The 05xx exchange compares a few numbers, not a list of transactions. Each end counts from its own records, independently, and one online message says within seconds whether a day needs work. A list of every transaction would be large, would travel in files, and would arrive after the money had moved.


Totals show only that two ends differ, not where, and two differences of opposite sign cancel (unit 15). The categories narrow the search: credits and debits, each with its reversals, so a difference in DE 77 alone points at reversals before anyone opens a file.
