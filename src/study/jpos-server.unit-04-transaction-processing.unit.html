At ten past three a cardholder's 60.00 of groceries is declined with 61, "exceeds amount limit". Her card allows 500.00 a day, and she hasn't bought anything today. Before lunch she tried a jacket for 300.00 and shoes for 150.00, both declined with 51 because her account holds 120.00. Our participant that counts each purchase against the daily limit counted those two as well, and nothing gave the amounts back.

Unit s1 showed how a participant votes in prepare and how SendResponse answers on either path. This unit covers what happens after the votes, and how long a transaction holds a session, in six rules, one per section heading from 4.2.

A Participant Hears the Outcome Only If It Joined

The descriptor behind the groceries story has two participants that decide and one that answers:

ReserveDailyLimit adds each purchase to the card's total for the day and declines with 61 when the total would pass 500.00. Authorize stands in for the account check and declines with 51 above 120.00. Both build answers with Responses.reply, and the totals live in a shared DailyTotals; both are in the Reference. The first ReserveDailyLimit (it ran as demo.LeakyReserveDailyLimit) ended its prepare like this:

Both votes say NO_JOIN: the participant had done its part and seemed to have nothing left to do. The cardholder's day, all on 4111111111111111, with the card's total after each answer:

Only SendResponse got an abort call: after the votes, the TransactionManager calls commit or abort only on the participants that joined. NO_JOIN doesn't mean "I'm done"; it means "don't tell me how this ends". A participant that changed something outside the Context in prepare must hear the outcome, because on the abort path it is the only one that knows what to undo.

The participant joins when it reserves, and leaves the amount in the Context for its abort:

The same day:

Joining depends on the flag, not on the vote: a participant that votes ABORTED also hears the abort unless it says NO_JOIN, as (1) does, and one that throws from prepare counts as a plain ABORTED and joins too. Had (1) voted plain ABORTED, abort would reach a participant that reserved nothing. The RESERVED check would save the total; an unchecked abort would subtract the amount of every purchase the limit declined.

With the leak, the cardholder notices first, at the till, and the total stays wrong until the daily reset. The log shows nothing wrong: every transaction ended the way its votes said.

After an Abort, Only Abort Participants Are Asked


For a card-testing alert (a stolen card number tried again and again until something goes through), the fraud team wants declines counted per card. The counting participant joins every transaction, counts in abort, and goes after Authorize in the chain of 4.2, once every decision is made:



Three purchases on 5555555555554444, all larger than the account can pay:



Unit s1 showed the reason in SendResponse: once a participant votes ABORTED, the TransactionManager offers the rest of the chain only to participants that implement AbortParticipant. The monitor was a plain participant, so its prepare never ran, it never joined, and its abort was never called; the trace names only ReserveDailyLimit and SendResponse. (The counts are a ConcurrentHashMap, registered under declines before Q2 starts, since every session updates it.)


The fix implements AbortParticipant instead of TransactionParticipant:




The end of the trace, after Authorize voted ABORTED:



On the abort path the TransactionManager calls prepareForAbort, whose default in AbortParticipant calls prepare. That is fine here, since this prepare decides nothing. A participant whose prepare does real work, such as reserving an amount, would do it on aborted transactions too; override prepareForAbort when the two paths need different things.


The plain monitor placed at the top of the chain, ahead of ReserveDailyLimit, also counted all three: it was prepared before anyone aborted, and it joined. A participant sees every outcome if it comes before every participant that can abort, or if it implements AbortParticipant.



With the plain monitor after Authorize, the alert never fires: a card under attack shows zero declines while the attacker works through amounts until one is approved.


Commit Cannot Change the Outcome


Settlement reconciles the day against a journal of approvals. RecordApproval joins when the response says 00 and writes the line in commit, as unit s1 recommends for work that must happen only if the whole transaction succeeds. It sits between Authorize and SendResponse:



The journal's disk is full, and every write throws. A purchase of 27.50:





By the time commit runs, every participant has voted PREPARED, and the TransactionManager won't go back on that. An exception from commit becomes a warning, and the next participant's commit runs. In 2.1.7 the calls go in declaration order, so SendResponse, declared last, sent the approval after RecordApproval had failed. abort is called in the same order and treats exceptions the same way.


The terminal printed a receipt, the money moved, and the journal has no line. Settlement is short by 27.50, and by every approval after it until someone frees the disk; the only trace is one <warn> per purchase.


Do what can fail in prepare, in a form abort can undo, while a failure can still become a decline; commit only makes it final. A failure after Authorize has put its 00 in RESPONSE becomes a decline only if something replaces that 00; otherwise the abort path sends it (unit s10). With a database, that means writing the row in prepare inside an open database transaction and committing it in commit (unit s9). A database commit can still fail, much more rarely, and then the TransactionManager can only log it, which is one reason settlement reconciles at all.


In jPOS 3 commit and abort are called in reverse declaration order.

A Session Is Busy for the Whole Transaction


The server now forwards purchases to the card issuer's host through a QMUX, issuer-mux (the client side of unit c12). Visa's issuer answers at once; today Mastercard's takes two seconds for every answer. The obvious participant asks and waits:



After it, AnswerFromIssuer builds the response from the issuer's field 39, or 91 (issuer unavailable) when there was none, and votes. The TransactionManager runs four sessions. Eight Mastercard purchases arrive within 50 ms, each from its own terminal, and 100 ms later one Visa purchase. How long does the Visa purchase wait?



A session takes a Context from the queue and keeps it until the last commit or abort returns. While mux.request waits, the session's thread waits with it. The first four Mastercard purchases held all four sessions for two seconds, the next four took them for the next two, and the Visa purchase waited in the queue behind them. Each transaction's event shows the queue at the moment it ended: outstanding counts the Contexts that no session has taken yet.



Four sessions, each held two seconds, finish two Mastercard purchases a second. We sent four a second, plus one Visa purchase a second, for 20 seconds:



The wait grows by a second every second. After 15 seconds it reaches the listener's timeout (15000 ms by default, unit s1), and Contexts start to expire from the queue before any session takes them. From then on the server answers some requests 14.7 seconds late and the others never, whatever their brand. Acquirers reverse the purchases their terminals gave up on, and the reversals join the same queue.


max-sessions lets the TransactionManager add sessions when the queue grows. In 2.1.7 each one is a platform thread, and a slow host holds every one you add. Size the sessions from the time a transaction holds one, and put a timeout shorter than the terminal's on every call a participant makes.


PAUSE Gives the Session Back While You Wait


QMUX can also call you back instead of blocking: mux.request(m, timeout, listener, handBack) returns at once and later calls listener.responseReceived(response, handBack), with null once the timeout passes. A participant that returns PAUSE tells the TransactionManager to set the transaction aside and free the session. ctx.resume() puts the Context back at the head of the queue, and the next free session continues with the participant after the one that paused:



The same nine purchases and four sessions:



The Visa purchase's event, written while the eight Mastercard purchases were paused:



Under the 20-second load of 4.6, all 80 Mastercard purchases were answered, and every Visa purchase in under 100 ms. A paused transaction holds no thread, so the limit is now the issuer and the MUX's timeout.


  • (1) and (2): the vote is cast before the answer exists. PREPARED | PAUSE says "go on when I resume". This participant can't change its vote once the issuer answers, so a participant after it decides, here AnswerFromIssuer.
  • (3): the callback finds its transaction through handBack. One QueryIssuer serves every session (unit s1), and dozens of transactions may be paused in it at once. A field holding "the current Context" would hold whichever transaction paused last.
  • (4): every callback resumes, the empty one too. When the timeout passes, QMUX calls the listener's expired, which by default calls responseReceived(null, ...).



With the issuer silent and a 3-second timeout:



AnswerFromIssuer found no issuer response and answered 91. A MUX that isn't deployed or isn't connected gets the same answer at once, from the check before (1). A transaction that is never resumed stays paused, and its terminal waits out its own timeout; the TransactionManager's pause-timeout property, when set, resumes it on the abort path after that many milliseconds.


jPOS's own org.jpos.transaction.participant.QueryHost does this, pausing while its continuations property is true, the default. It picks the MUX from a destination in the Context and reports a timeout through ctx.getResult(); unit s5 uses it. Join is the other tool for waiting: it runs the participants nested in it in parallel, for independent slow steps such as a call to an HSM and a database lookup.


In jPOS 3 sessions run on virtual threads. A blocked session costs little, and PAUSE is deprecated.

Summary


  • A participant hears the outcome only if it joined. One that changes something outside the Context in prepare joins and undoes it in abort; with NO_JOIN what it reserved leaks. One that votes ABORTED joins too, unless it says NO_JOIN.
  • After an abort, only abort participants are asked. Plain participants later in the chain are never prepared and never hear the abort. prepareForAbort calls prepare unless you override it.
  • Commit cannot change the outcome. An exception in commit is a warning, and the approval still goes out. Do what can fail in prepare, in a form abort can undo.
  • The Context is keyed by strings. ctx.get(REQUEST) returns null; use REQUEST.toString().
  • A session is busy for the whole transaction. Sessions divided by the time each transaction holds one is the throughput. Past it the queue grows until the listener's timeout drops requests, slow and fast alike.
  • PAUSE gives the session back. The callback finds its Context through handBack and always resumes, and a participant after the paused one decides.

Who is called (2.1.7): until someone aborts, prepare on each participant in order; after that, only prepareForAbort on AbortParticipants. Then commit (everyone PREPARED) or abort on those that joined, in declaration order. A throw from prepare counts as ABORTED and joins; a throw from prepareForAbort counts as ABORTED | NO_JOIN; a throw from commit or abort is logged and the next participant is called.


Participant lifecycle: created once, from class, with its public no-argument constructor; then setLogger if it is a LogSource, setTransactionManager(TransactionManager) if it has that method, and setConfiguration if it is Configurable. A ConfigurationException there stops the TransactionManager from starting (unit s1). destroy() is called at stop if it implements org.jpos.util.Destroyable. On a session thread, TransactionManager.getContext() and getId() return the current transaction's Context and id.

Design Note: Why Does the TransactionManager Ask Twice?


A TransactionManager could run each participant once and stop at the first failure. jPOS asks in two rounds instead, the way a database coordinates a distributed commit: first every participant says whether it can, then each one that joined is told what happened.


The reason is the late decline. In an authorization the step that says no is often not the first: the limit and the card are fine, and then the account check or the issuer declines. Every step before it may have changed something, such as a reserved amount, a counter or a row, and each has to undo exactly what it did. Two rounds let each participant keep its undo next to its own work, and the TransactionManager only has to remember who joined.


The price is discipline the TransactionManager can't enforce. It doesn't know what a participant changed, so it believes the flags: after NO_JOIN it never calls that participant again, and after READONLY it skips the snapshot. And once everyone has voted PREPARED it has no way back, so a commit that fails can only be logged.
