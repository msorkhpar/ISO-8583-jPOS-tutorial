A payment switch went live with a new release on Saturday. On Monday morning three tickets are waiting:

TicketWhat happened
1The acquirer's analyst writes that purchase STAN 000731 from terminal 29110001, sent at 10:15:00 on 7 November, was rejected with 30, format error
2Every purchase from the new terminal 29110007 times out, while the acquirer's portal shows each one approved and then reversed
3Saturday's total for the shop's terminals agrees with the acquirer's settlement report to the cent, yet one merchant is 30.00 short and one cardholder was charged 30.00 for a purchase the terminal reported as failed

None of them says what is wrong. Getting from a ticket to the cause comes down to four rules, one per section heading. Every output comes from jPOS 2.1.7, with messages packed by iso87ascii.xml. Units c17 and s8 show the jPOS tools for the same work.

A Message Is Named by Its Terminal, STAN and Transmission Time, and the Time Is UTC

Before reading any bytes, find ticket 1's message in your own log: it says which code built the message and what it was meant to carry. The switch runs in Berlin and stamps its log in local time. Searched by the STAN, by the analyst's time, and by fields 41, 11 and 7 together, the log gives:

The STAN alone finds three purchases and their answers. Each terminal numbers its own messages and restarts at 000001 every morning, so every busy terminal sends a 000731 every day.

The analyst's time finds an approved purchase from another terminal. The analyst read 10:15:00 from field 7, the transmission date and time, which the standard keeps in UTC (GMT in the 1987 text). The log's 10:15 is Berlin time, an hour ahead in November. Fields 12 and 13 hold the terminal's local time, so a search on them fails the same way. Answer the analyst from that line, "our purchase was approved", and the ticket goes back and forth for a day while every purchase like 000731 keeps failing.

Terminal, STAN and transmission time together find one request and its response. A response echoes fields 11 and 41, most networks echo 7 as well, and the MTI tells the two apart. A reversal names its original in field 90 (unit 4) by MTI, STAN and transmission time, with the acquirer and forwarder IDs (fields 32 and 33) in place of the terminal.

Field 11 names a message on one link only. The acquirer's host forwards the purchase to the network under a STAN of its own (unit c12), so the issuer's staff won't find 000731. Ask them by the retrieval reference number, field 37, which the acquirer's side assigns (the terminal or the acquirer's host) and the hops after it usually pass on unchanged. The Reference lists what a ticket should carry, in either direction.

A Rejected Message Is Compared With Approved Ones by the Shape of Each Field


The analyst attached the bytes (unit 10). Read them back with your packager. Code 30 doesn't say which field the host disliked, so compare the message with one the host approved: a purchase from another terminal twelve minutes earlier, after the same release. Comparing the values of every field gives:



All ten would differ between two healthy purchases as well: another card, amount, time, entry mode, terminal and merchant. A format error is about the form of a value, so compare each field's shape instead: whether it is there, its length, and what kind of characters it holds.




Field 43, the merchant's name and location, now holds a character outside printable ASCII. The release started sending names as the merchants typed them, and this one is CAFÉ AM SEE. The packager wrote É as the single byte C9 (ISO-8859-1), because it checks lengths and never characters (unit 3). Field 43 is ans, and specifications take its special characters from printable ASCII, so the host refuses C9. Every merchant with an accent in its name was rejected all weekend. The others were approved, which is why the release passed its tests.


Compare with the nearest approved message: the same release, the same kind of purchase. A message from before the release differs in everything the release changed.


If the host's packager parses your bytes differently from your own, its error names a field after the wrong one (unit 3). Start from the first field whose shape changed.

A Missing Answer Is Placed by the Last Log That Saw the Message


For ticket 2, a longer timeout or sending each purchase again are the obvious fixes. Before changing anything, find the last log that shows the message. An unanswered request stopped in one of four places:


  • It never left. Your log shows it built but no bytes written: the link was down, or the packager refused a value (unit 3). The host knows nothing of it, so there is nothing to undo, and once the cause is fixed the purchase can go as a new message.
  • It was lost between the two logs, on the way to the host or on the way back. Your log shows the bytes written, and the host's shows nothing, or an answer you never received. A host's log that shows an answer sent says nothing about your side until your own log shows it arrived.
  • The host received it and never answered. Its log shows the request and nothing after it.
  • The answer reached you and nothing took it. It arrived after your timeout, or it didn't match the request it answers.


In the last three the request left, and nobody on your side knows what the issuer decided. The purchase is reversed (unit 4) in all three, even when the host's log turns out to show nothing: a reversal of a purchase the host never saw is acknowledged and changes nothing. The switch has to reverse at the timeout, long before anyone can read another company's log. The place decides what you fix: the link, the host, your timeout (unit c12), or the way you pair answers with requests.


Spot the Problem: The Terminal Whose Approvals Never Arrived


The switch pairs each answer with its request on the MTI and fields 41, 11 and 37 (unit c12 shows why a link adds 37). A purchase from an older terminal and one from terminal 29110007, each with the acquirer's answer. The key starts with the MTI without its last digit, mapped so that 0200 and 0210 share it:



Why does the second answer never find its request?


The older terminals fill field 37 themselves. The new model leaves it out, as the acquirer's specification allows, and the acquirer's host assigns a reference number in its answer. The request's key has two pairing fields and the response's has three. The approval arrives within a second, matches no waiting request and is dropped. The purchase times out and is reversed: the host undoes an approval, the cardholder is told the payment failed, and the merchant loses every sale on that terminal. Neither obvious fix helps: a longer timeout changes nothing, and sending again would add a second purchase whenever an answer really was lost.


Compare the request as it left with the response as it arrived, on the pairing fields. A field the response adds, drops or rewrites (a reference number assigned by the host, trailing spaces trimmed from field 37, a STAN renumbered by a hop) breaks the pairing. Pair only on fields that every request carries and the other side echoes unchanged. QMUX also leaves absent fields out of its key (unit c12).


A Difference in Totals Is Explained Transaction by Transaction, Even When the Totals Agree


Ticket 3: Saturday's total for the shop's terminals agrees with the acquirer's settlement report. Is Saturday right?


The two lists, matched by terminal and retrieval reference number, in euros:



Two transactions are wrong, by 30.00 in opposite directions, and the totals hide both:


  • 802 was answered 10, a partial approval of 50.00 out of 80.00 (unit 9). The switch booked the requested amount and told the terminal "approved". The issuer charged the card 50.00, since the switch's booking moves no money, so the merchant handed over 80.00 of goods and will be paid 50.00.
  • 804 timed out, and the switch queued a reversal that never reached the host. The terminal showed "failed", the cardholder paid another way, and the issuer charged the card for 30.00 as well.


A day that balances can hold any number of such pairs, so match every transaction every day, not only on days whose totals differ. The key is the terminal with the RRN, because an RRN is unique only within the system that assigned it (unit 19). The size of a difference usually points at its cause; the Reference lists the common ones. Unit 19 matches clearing files the same way.

Summary


  • A message is named by its terminal, STAN and transmission time. STANs repeat across terminals and days, and field 7 is UTC while your log and fields 12 and 13 are local, so a search on any one of them can find another message. Across hops, ask by the RRN.
  • A rejected message is compared with approved ones by shape: presence, length and kind of characters. Values differ between any two purchases; a shape no approved message has is the format error.
  • A missing answer is placed by the last log that saw it. A request that left is reversed wherever it stopped, and the place says what to fix. An answer that adds or changes a pairing field matches nothing and looks like silence.
  • A difference in totals is explained transaction by transaction. Equal totals can hide differences that cancel, so match every day by terminal and RRN.

What a ticket carries, whichever side writes it:


  • the message's MTI, terminal (41), STAN (11) and transmission time (7, in UTC), and the RRN (37);
  • the bytes that left as a text hex dump (unit 10), not a screenshot or a whole network capture;
  • test cards only, or the card data masked before the dump leaves the cardholder data environment (unit 13);
  • what was expected and what came back: field 39, or nothing;
  • the specification release, the packager in use, and what changed recently.


Challenges


  1. An analyst writes only "STAN 000731, about 10:15". On your busiest day, how many messages could that be, and what would you ask for before you search?
  2. A host echoes fields 11 and 41 but rewrites field 37 in some answers. Which fields would you pair on, and what would you log so that the next missing answer is placed in minutes?
  3. Comparing shapes flags field 2 because approved purchases carry 16-digit cards and the rejected one a 19-digit card. How would you collect the shapes seen in a week of approved traffic without storing a card number?
  4. Your team reconciles only when the totals differ. Estimate from your own traffic how many offsetting pairs like 802 and 804 go unseen in a month, and who pays for each.


Design Note: Why a Purchase Carries Three Dates


A purchase at 00:30 on 1 November in Berlin carries three dates. Fields 12 and 13 say 00:30 on 1 November, the terminal's clock, the time printed on the receipt. Field 7 says 23:30 on 31 October, in UTC, the one clock every party on the path can compare. Field 15, the settlement date, names the network's business day, which its cutover time decides (unit 19).


Each date answers a different party's question. The cardholder disputes by the receipt, the networks trace a message by its transmission time, and money moves by the settlement date. A single date would follow one party's clock, and every other party would convert it in its own way.


Every search has to know which date it holds, and a report that mixes them puts purchases on the wrong day. None of the three carries a year (unit 12).
