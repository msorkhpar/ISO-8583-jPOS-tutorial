  16. Check yourself: EMV and DE 55 in depth

A cardholder pays 80.00 by chip. The issuer approves, and the acquirer's host logs the approval. The terminal prints DECLINED, the merchant keeps the goods, and the next morning the cardholder's banking app shows 80.00 held. Every host on the path did what its code says. What went wrong, and who has to put it right?

DE 55 carries the exchange between card, terminal and issuer, and the answer is in it. Unit 3 split DE 55 into tag-length-value objects, and unit 14 showed what the card's cryptogram proves and why nobody on the path may change the data it covers. Six rules follow, one per section heading. EMVCo defines the common part; your scheme's documents define its cryptogram versions, its layout of tag 9F10 and its own tags.

Only an ARQC Asks the Issuer, and the Card Keeps the Last Word

Once the terminal has read the card and run its own checks (the Reference lists the steps), it asks the card for a cryptogram. This is the first GENERATE AC command, and the terminal names the cryptogram it wants. The card applies its own risk rules and may answer with that one or a more cautious one, never a bolder one. It puts the cryptogram in tag 9F26 and says which one it is in the top two bits of tag 9F27, the cryptogram information data:

  • ARQC (bits 10): the card wants the issuer to decide. The terminal sends an authorization request with DE 55.
  • TC (bits 01): the card approves offline. No request goes out; the TC reaches the issuer later, in clearing (unit 19).
  • AAC (bits 00): the card declines, and the purchase ends at the counter.

After the issuer answers an ARQC, the terminal gives the card the response's chip data and asks a second time. At this second GENERATE AC the card returns a TC to complete the purchase or an AAC to decline it. After an issuer decline the card must decline too, but after an approval it can still decline. Section "A Card That Declines After an Approval Leaves an Approval to Reverse" shows what that costs.

Here is the chip data of the 80.00 purchase, printed with the tag names jPOS 2.1.7 knows, followed by four values of 9F27:

Most of DE 55 is what the card computed its cryptogram over, because the issuer needs the same inputs to check it (18.4). The rest says what the terminal and the card saw (18.3).

Bit 4 of 9F27 asks the terminal to send the issuer an advice (a report of what happened), and bits 3 to 1 give a reason (Reference). So 0B, at the second GENERATE AC, is an AAC that asks for an advice because issuer authentication failed. Code that tests cid == 0x00 for a decline files that card's decline as something else. Compare the top two bits only.

EMVStandardTagType.forCode throws a checked UnknownTagNumberException for a tag it doesn't list. Scheme tags such as DF71 are normal, so catch it. DE 55 can also hold the PAN (tag 5A) and track-equivalent data (tag 57): outside a test, print tag names and lengths, never values, and wipe DE 55 in logs (unit c8).

Chip Values Are BCD Digits, and Each Must Agree With the ISO Field It Mirrors

Four values travel twice in a chip authorization: in the ISO fields, which every host reads, and in DE 55, where the cryptogram covers them.

tagISO field (1987)they agree when
9F02 amount, authorisedDE 4the 12 digits are equal
5F2A transaction currencyDE 495F2A is 0 followed by the three digits of DE 49
9C transaction typeDE 39C equals the first two digits of DE 3
9A transaction date, YYMMDDDE 13, MMDDthe last four digits of 9A equal DE 13

The restaurant in unit 14 changed field 4 and left 9F02 alone. The cryptogram still matched, because it doesn't cover field 4, and only this comparison shows that the card agreed to a different amount. Issuers and networks run this check. An acquirer that runs it too finds its own bugs before certification. Here is the amount of a 125.50 purchase, read three ways:

EMV writes its numeric values (format n) as BCD: two decimal digits per byte, so the bytes 00 00 00 01 25 50 hold the digits 000000012550. ISOUtil.hexString gives you those digits. Read the six bytes as a binary number and 125.50 becomes 750.88. A host that does this reports every chip purchase as tampered with, and if it declines on a mismatch, it declines them all.

Searching the hex text is the subtler bug. A cryptogram is eight random-looking bytes, and here it happens to contain 9F 02 06, so the search took part of the cryptogram for the amount. Random bytes contain that pattern only about once in a million purchases, so no test finds it, and production declines a purchase now and then that nobody can reproduce. Find a tag by parsing the list, as unit 3 does.

Both amounts are minor units of the same currency (unit 11), so 1500 yen is 000000001500 in both. Compare only what both sides carry: a tag or a field that is missing is a matter for the message's mandatory-field rules (unit 3), not a disagreement.

The TVR Says What the Terminal Saw, and Tag 9F10 What the Card Saw


The terminal verification results (tag 95) are five bytes of flags the terminal sets during the transaction: one byte for offline data authentication, one for processing restrictions, one for cardholder verification, one for its risk management, one for issuer authentication and scripts. The Reference lists the flags. jPOS 2.1.7 names each one with a method:




When a chip purchase is declined in testing, read the TVR first. This one says the card's static data failed authentication and the amount was over the terminal's floor limit. The EMV books number bits from 8, the leftmost and most significant, down to 1. So "byte 1, bit 7" is the mask 0x40. Write it as 1 << 7 and you test 0x80, which is bit 8, "offline data authentication was not performed". A risk rule meant to decline cards that failed authentication then fires on a different condition and lets every real failure through. Use the named methods, or masks written as EMV counts.


The issuer application data (tag 9F10) is data the card adds for its issuer, in a layout each scheme defines. It usually holds a key index, the cryptogram version number (CVN), which tells the issuer how to check the cryptogram, and the card verification results (CVR): whether the card checked a PIN offline, which cryptogram it returned, whether its last online transaction failed issuer authentication. IssuerApplicationData guesses the layout from the length and, for some layouts, the first bytes, and takes the CVN and the CVR from different positions for each. Use the guess when debugging, and take the real layout from the scheme. CVRMastercard decodes Mastercard's CVR.


A Valid Cryptogram Proves the Card, Not That the Request Is New


The issuer checks the ARQC in its HSM (unit 8), because its keys exist in clear only there; outside, they are stored encrypted under the HSM's master key, the LMK:


  1. From its issuer master key for cryptograms (IMK-AC), the PAN and the PAN sequence number (tag 5F34, DE 23), the HSM derives the key of this one card. EMV calls this master key derivation, and option A is the common method.
  2. From the card key and the transaction counter (9F36), it derives a session key for this transaction. Whether there is a session key, and how it is derived, depends on the CVN: the sample card's CVN 12 (18) uses EMV's common session key.
  3. It computes a MAC (ISO 9797-1 algorithm 3, as in unit 14) over the covered data, in the order the card's CVN defines, and compares it with 9F26.
  4. If they match, it computes the response cryptogram, the ARPC (18.5).


jPOS's SMAdapter does all four in verifyARQCGenerateARPC, which returns the ARPC, or null when the cryptogram is wrong:



To have a card, we let jPOS's software security module play one. Its calculateARQC is protected and documented as an entry point for simulators, so a subclass exposes it. The keys are test keys that everyone knows:



The card, with PAN sequence number (PSN) 01, computes the ARQC of the 80.00 purchase. The issuer verifies the request three times: as sent, when the same bytes arrive a second time, and after a switch on the way dropped DE 23. It keeps the last counter it verified for each card:




The copy verifies. The cryptogram covers the counter and the terminal's unpredictable number, and a copy carries both unchanged, so the HSM computes the same MAC and accepts the same bytes again. Anyone who captured one authorization could replay it from another merchant. The cryptographic check that catches it is the issuer's own record: the last ATC it verified for this card. A legitimate repeat (an 0101 after a timeout) carries the same counter too, so match repeats of a message you have already seen (by STAN and RRN) first. Any other counter that repeats or goes backwards is a replay or a cloned card. A large jump can be honest, after offline purchases, and the issuer's rules decide how large is too large. The ATC is binary (format b), not BCD: 0021 is 33.


Record the counter only after the cryptogram verified. A forged request carrying ATC FFFF fails the HSM, but a host that stored its counter first now refuses every genuine purchase on that card.


The card key needs the PAN sequence number. Two cards with the same PAN, an old one and its replacement, have different keys because their sequence numbers differ. jPOS's software module treats a missing sequence number as 00, so a switch that drops DE 23 fails every card with PSN 01 and passes every test card with 00.


null is a decline. The same input gives the same answer, so retrying it is pointless, and approving in its place (stand-in) approves exactly the cards the check exists to stop. Networks define a response code for a cryptogram failure.

The Response Carries the ARPC in Tag 91 and the Scripts as TLV Inside TLV


The issuer's answer can carry chip data of its own, in the DE 55 of the 0110 or 0210:


  • Tag 91, issuer authentication data. In method 1 it is the 8-byte ARPC followed by the 2-byte authorisation response code (ARC), such as ASCII 00. The ARPC is the ARQC combined with the ARC and encrypted with the card's key for this transaction, so the card can see that its own issuer saw this ARQC and gave this answer. Method 2, chosen by the cryptogram version, has another layout (Reference).
  • Issuer scripts: commands for the card, such as unblocking a PIN or the application, or changing a limit. Template 71 runs before the second GENERATE AC, so it can change the card's decision in this purchase. Template 72 runs after it, for the next purchase. Inside a template come an optional script identifier (9F18, 4 bytes) and one or more commands (86), each protected by a MAC that the issuer's HSM computes (SMAdapter.generateSM_MAC).


A template is a constructed object: bit 6 of its first tag byte (0x20) is set, and its value is a TLV list itself. TLVList doesn't descend into it, so you pack the inner list and append it as the template's value. We put the ARPC from 18.4 into tag 91 and add a script for after the final GENERATE AC:




Split up, that is 91 0A and ten bytes (the ARPC, then 3030), then 72 16 and 22 bytes, which are 9F18 04 00000001 and 86 0D followed by the 13-byte command. The template's length counts its encoded elements, tags and lengths included, not only the command bytes. Write 72 0D instead and the template ends in the middle of the 86 element: the terminal can't parse it, and the script never runs. The second template holds 144 bytes, so its length takes the long form, 81 90, as in unit 3.


The terminal reports how the scripts went in byte 5 of this purchase's TVR and, on many networks, in a script results tag (often 9F5B). Both reach the issuer in a later message about this purchase, such as the clearing record, an advice or a reversal. A script whose result never comes back is usually sent again. Terminals and networks limit how many script bytes a response may carry, so keep scripts short.

A Card That Declines After an Approval Leaves an Approval to Reverse


Back to the 80.00 purchase. The issuer approved it with response code 08, "honour with identification", and built tag 91 for the ARC 08. The acquirer's gateway translates 08 to 00 for its terminals, which know only 00 (unit 9), and to keep DE 55 "consistent" it rewrote the ARC inside tag 91 as well. The card checks tag 91 by computing the ARPC itself, from its own key and the ARC it received:




With the ARC rewritten, the ARPC no longer matches: to the card, the answer did not come from its issuer. Issuer authentication has failed, the terminal records that in byte 5 of the TVR, and at the second GENERATE AC the card applies the rule its issuer gave it. Many cards are set to decline then, and this one returned an AAC. The terminal printed DECLINED.


The issuer has approved 80.00 and holds it against the cardholder's balance. Only the acquirer knows the purchase failed, so it must undo the approval with a reversal for the authorization (unit 4; unit c12 builds one), as for any approval the merchant could not complete. Many networks want the card's final cryptogram and TVR in the DE 55 of that reversal, or of an advice, so the issuer can see why. Sending the authorization again doesn't help: the card's decline is final, and a new request would be a second purchase while the first approval still holds the money. Without the reversal, the hold stays until it expires, and the cardholder calls the bank.



A hop that drops DE 55 from the response, like the gateway in unit 14 that copies a fixed list of fields, leaves the card no ARPC, and whether it then declines depends on its issuer's settings. Pass DE 55 through unchanged in both directions. Translate DE 39 if your terminals need it, and leave tag 91 alone.


Contactless. Most contactless kernels produce the cryptogram in one tap, and the card leaves the field before the answer arrives. There is no second GENERATE AC, so a tag 91 or a script in the response, template 71 or 72 alike, usually can't reach the card in this purchase, and the issuer's answer stands. DE 22 says contactless chip (07x in common 1987 usage, unit 14), and the issuer verifies the cryptogram as for contact. Each scheme's kernel adds tags of its own.


Fallback. When the chip can't be read, the terminal may read the magnetic stripe of the same card (DE 22 80x). Such a request carries no DE 55 and no cryptogram, so issuers treat it as high risk, and many decline it.


Summary


  • Only an ARQC asks the issuer, and the card keeps the last word. The top two bits of 9F27 say which cryptogram the card made; testing the whole byte misfiles cryptograms whose low bits are set.
  • Chip values are BCD digits, and each must agree with its ISO field. 9F02, 5F2A, 9C and 9A mirror DE 4, DE 49, DE 3 and DE 13. Read as binary or found by searching the hex, they report mismatches that aren't there.
  • The TVR says what the terminal saw, and 9F10 what the card saw. EMV numbers bits from 8 down to 1, and the layout of 9F10 belongs to the scheme.
  • A valid cryptogram proves the card, not that the request is new. A replayed request verifies again; only the last ATC verified per card catches it. A missing PAN sequence number fails every card that has one, and a failed cryptogram is a decline.
  • The response carries the ARPC in tag 91 and scripts as TLV inside TLV. A template's length counts its encoded elements.
  • A card that declines after an approval leaves an approval to reverse. A hop that changes or drops tag 91 can make the card refuse its issuer's answer, and the acquirer must then reverse.

Script templates: 71 before and 72 after the second GENERATE AC; inside, 9F18 (4 bytes, optional), then one 86 per command.


jPOS 2.1.7: TLVList and TLVMsg (org.jpos.tlv) split and build the list. TLVList.pack() fails past 516 bytes, which a response's DE 55 rarely reaches (concatenating each TLVMsg.getTLV() has no limit). In the 2023 edition DE 55 stays raw BER-TLV, without the dataset envelope other composite fields have (unit 17). EMVStandardTagType, TerminalVerificationResults, IssuerApplicationData and CVRMastercard (org.jpos.emv) name tags and flags. SMAdapter.verifyARQC, generateARPC, verifyARQCGenerateARPC and generateSM_MAC run in the HSM, with MKDMethod, SKDMethod and ARPCMethod choosing the derivations.


Challenges


  1. Your issuer sees ATC jumps of 40 or more on about one card in a thousand. Which of them are offline purchases and which might be clones? Decide what data you would need, and what threshold you would set.
  2. A network lets the issuer answer a contactless purchase with a script that the card can only receive at its next contact purchase. Design how the issuer keeps the script pending, and decide when it gives up.
  3. Your acquirer host could check 9F02 against DE 4 itself, or leave it to the issuer. Count what each choice costs when a terminal model starts sending a wrong 9F02 for every tipped purchase.
  4. Write down every place in your switch that copies a field list, translates a value or rebuilds DE 55. For each one, decide whether it can change a byte the card or the issuer checks.


Design Note: Why the Card Has the Last Word


EMV was designed for a time when many terminals were offline most of the day. A card had to be able to approve or decline by itself, so it carries its own counters, its own limits and its own risk rules, and the issuer can change them only through scripts. The online exchange keeps part of that design: an issuer decline is final, but an issuer approval still needs the card's agreement, and the ARPC is how the card knows the approval is genuine.


The cost is that two parties decide one purchase. When the card declines what the issuer approved, the terminal follows the card while the issuer has already reserved the money, and every such case needs a reversal that the acquirer has to send and the issuer has to match. Contactless dropped the second GENERATE AC for speed, and with it, in most purchases, the card's chance to disagree.
